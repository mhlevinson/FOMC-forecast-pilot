# F04 forecast: FOMC decision, 8-9 December 2026

Based only on the evidence pack cp/K1/pack.md (22 official documents, captured 2026-11-26). The upper bound before the meeting is 4.00 percent (target range 3-3/4 to 4 percent).

## 1. What the Committee did and what it is signalling

**Recent actions**
- September 15-16: raised the range 25 bp to 3-3/4 to 4 percent ("removed a dose of accommodation").
- October 27-28: held, by a 10-2 vote. Cook and Hammack dissented in favour of a 25 bp hike.

**September SEP dots for end-2026** (midpoint is now 3.875)
- 12 of 18 at 4.125, which is one more hike this year.
- 4 of 18 at 4.375, which is two more.
- 2 of 18 at 3.875, which is no more.
- December is the only meeting left, so 16 of 18 had at least one more hike landing at this meeting.

**October minutes**
- "Almost all participants" judged that further increases "broadly consistent with the September SEP" would likely be appropriate.
- "Some" said market pricing of tightening "in December and next year" matched their own view of the appropriate path, and several saw that as what made holding in October low-risk.
- "Some" wanted to hike in October already, and "a few" who held called it a close call.
- "A couple" said more than 25 bp could warrant consideration in December if inflation data were unfavourable.
- Futures put a high probability on a 25 bp hike by year-end; the Desk survey median had a December hike.
- The statement language was "accumulation of more information" and "The Committee will deliver price stability."

**Members since the meeting**
- Waller (Nov 10): tightening needed "sooner rather than later"; he would support 25 bp in December even if October inflation improved. If core CPI came in "around 0.3 percent or even higher", 50 bp "should be considered" and "hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk". He also said promised hikes must be carried out in reasonable time or markets will unwind them.
- Cook (Nov 20): dissented for a hike, says "waiting has a cost", and is sceptical that one hike is enough.
- Barr (Nov 18): expects "it will be appropriate to raise rates further".
- Jefferson (Nov 13): inflation gives "the more important signal"; data since October show "persistent price pressures".
- Bowman (Nov 10): the most cautious voice. The benefit of more tightening "has to be carefully weighed against its costs" for rate-sensitive sectors, and financial conditions have tightened. But she also says inflation is persisting and broadening, the labour market is firming, and "there is still work to do".
- Warsh (Oct 28 press conference): refuses forward guidance and will not prejudge. He stresses "we don't just talk, we act", that tighter financial conditions mean "policy is working", and that the coming interval is "information rich".

The October hold reads as a skip inside a tightening cycle, not a stop.

## 2. Latest official data and which way each pushes

**Inflation: pushes toward a hike, with some push toward a larger one**
- October CPI: headline +0.4 percent, core +0.3 percent for the third straight month. That is exactly the threshold Waller named for considering 50 bp.
- October PCE: +0.3 percent headline and core on the month; 3.8 percent headline and 3.3 percent core over 12 months, with core up from 3.1 percent in September.
- Breadth is widening (services, transportation services, recreation, medical care), per the CPI detail and several speeches.
- Beige Book: prices up moderately, wage growth ticked up to moderate, and firms expect further increases.
- Mild offset: oil fell sharply in early November on hopes of a US-Iran agreement, and gasoline was up only 1.0 percent in October after 9.1 percent in September.

**Employment: removes any labour-market reason to wait**
- October payrolls +165,000, with August and September revised up a combined 84,000.
- Unemployment 4.1 percent; average hourly earnings +0.4 percent on the month and 3.1 percent over 12 months.
- Soft spots: involuntary part-time work rose by 339,000, long-term unemployment is 27.6 percent of the unemployed, and hiring is low.

**Activity: strong in aggregate, softer at the margin**
- Q3 GDP revised up to 4.3 percent from 3.4 percent; real final sales to private domestic purchasers 5.8 percent.
- Softer: real PCE flat in October, and the Beige Book shows consumer spending little changed with housing, construction and autos weak.
- Financial conditions have tightened: wider credit spreads, higher mortgage rates, and AI equity valuations down over "the past couple of weeks" as of mid-November.
- The softer items argue against 50 bp and give a small opening for a hold, but not for easing.

## 3. Strongest case for each outcome

- **Cut of 50 bp or more:** only a sudden systemic event before December 9, such as a disorderly AI or hedge-fund deleveraging that impairs market functioning, or a cyber or banking shock. With inflation at 3.8 percent the Fed would reach for liquidity tools before rate cuts. Near zero.
- **Cut of 25 bp:** the same crisis path in milder form, or an abrupt collapse in activity. No participant in the pack contemplates easing. Near zero.
- **Hold:** Warsh will not pre-commit and already skipped once despite a hawkish majority. Financial conditions have tightened, AI-related markets are wobbling, the Financial Stability Report flags amplification risks, oil has fallen on peace hopes, real spending stalled in October, and Bowman wants the costs weighed. A sharp equity or credit selloff or a weak November jobs report (due December 4) could make "accumulate more information" the path of least resistance again. Political pressure against hikes is visible in the pack.
- **Hike of 25 bp:** the modal case by a wide margin. It matches the September dots, the "almost all participants" language in the minutes, and market and Desk survey expectations as of late October. Waller, Cook, Barr and Hammack lean explicitly that way, and Jefferson implicitly. Every data release since October has been hot on inflation and solid on jobs and growth. Skipping twice would unwind priced-in tightening, which Waller explicitly warns against, and would undercut "we will deliver price stability". A 25 bp step also fits the "gradual approach" and the tightening in financial conditions.
- **Hike of 50 bp or more:** Waller's stated trigger was met by the 0.3 percent core CPI. Core PCE rose to 3.3 percent, GDP was revised to 4.3 percent, and payrolls were strong. "A couple" of participants already floated more than 25 bp. Having skipped October, the Committee could "catch up" with 50, and Warsh's rhetoric allows for a bold move.

## 4. What I am least sure of

- **Warsh's reaction function.** He is new, rejects forward guidance, and the pack has no post-October policy signal from him, Williams, Powell, Logan, Kashkari or Paulson. This widens both tails, hold and 50 bp.
- **Financial-market conditions between November 26 and December 9.** The pack shows ongoing AI-related volatility but no current market pricing after the CPI and Waller's speech. A disorderly selloff is the main route to a hold, which keeps hold above 10 percent.
- **How many members share Waller's 50 bp view.** The core CPI trigger was met only at the threshold, not above it. The October hold majority, the "gradual" language, tighter financial conditions and flat real spending all argue for 25 bp, with Waller possibly dissenting for more. I keep 50 bp near 10 percent rather than higher.
- **Remaining data.** The November jobs report on December 4 could shift the hold and 50 bp tails at the margin. November CPI arrives December 10, after the decision.
- **Net effect.** These uncertainties take probability from the 25 bp hike and spread it roughly evenly to hold and 50 bp. They do not make any cut plausible.
