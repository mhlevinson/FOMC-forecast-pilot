# F10 - FOMC decision, 8-9 December 2026

Bottom line: a 25 basis point hike is the most likely outcome (about two in three). The main alternatives are a second straight hold (about one in six) and a 50 basis point hike (about one in seven). Cuts are a remote tail.

Everything below comes from the evidence pack only. Facts are stated as facts; my inferences are marked "Inference".

## 1. What the Committee did recently and what it signals

Facts
- September 15-16: raised the target range 25 bp to 3.75-4.00 percent (the "dose of accommodation" removed). Upper bound now 4.00.
- October 27-28: held at 3.75-4.00 by a 10-2 vote. Cook and Hammack dissented in favor of a 25 bp hike. The statement said more information "will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate" and "The Committee will deliver price stability."
- October minutes: "almost all participants" judged that further action to raise the range, broadly consistent with the September SEP, "would likely be appropriate." "Some participants" wanted to hike in October; "a few" holders called it a close call; "a couple" said more than 25 bp could warrant consideration in December if inflation data were unfavorable. Some said market pricing of a December step matched their own view, and several saw that alignment as lowering the cost of waiting.
- September dot plot, end-2026 midpoint: 12 at 4.125 (one more 25 bp hike from here), 4 at 4.375 (50 bp more), 2 at 3.875 (no more). So 16 of 18 had at least one more hike in 2026, and December is the last meeting of the year.
- Market pricing at the October meeting: high probability of a 25 bp hike by year-end; Desk survey median had a December hike.
- Speeches since:
  - Waller (10 Nov, before CPI): would support 25 bp in December even if October inflation improved; if October core CPI came in "around 0.3 percent or even higher", a 50 bp hike "should be considered" and "hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk." He also said promised hikes must be carried out in a reasonable time or policy loosens by default.
  - Cook (20 Nov, after CPI): repeated her dissent logic, said waiting has a cost, and that data since September "fail to provide me with sufficient evidence" of disinflation. Mentions "number and magnitude" of future adjustments but does not name 50.
  - Barr (18 Nov): "I expect it will be appropriate to raise rates further" if his forecast holds.
  - Jefferson (13 Nov): inflation trajectory is "the more important signal"; no explicit commitment.
  - Bowman (10 Nov): inflation more persistent than she expected and "still work to do", but the benefit of more tightening "has to be carefully weighed against its costs for interest-rate-sensitive sectors"; watching recent market volatility.
  - Warsh (28 Oct press conference, 16 Nov remarks): refuses forward guidance and will not prejudge December; says the coming period is "information rich"; comfortable that tighter financial conditions show "policy is working."

Inference
- The October hold was framed as a pause inside a tightening path, not the end of it. The center of the Committee has pre-announced a December step about as clearly as a no-guidance chair allows.

## 2. Latest official data and which way each pushes

Inflation - pushes toward a hike, and toward the larger one
- October CPI: headline +0.4 percent m/m (after +0.6); core +0.3 percent for the third straight month. Broad gains: food +0.5, transportation services +0.8, recreation +0.8, shelter +0.3. Used cars -1.2.
- September PCE: +0.5 m/m, 3.6 percent y/y; core +0.3 m/m, 3.1 percent y/y. Officials' estimates for October: headline about 3.8 percent, core 3.2-3.3 percent - moving up, and above the September SEP median path for core (3.4 Q4/Q4 is still roughly consistent, but with no sign of turning).
- Fact: the core CPI print of 0.3 meets the condition Waller set for putting 50 bp on the table.
- Offsetting fact: energy rose only 0.7 percent in October after 5.2, and Bowman reports Brent fell sharply in early November on hopes of a U.S.-Iran agreement.

Employment - pushes toward a hike (removes any labor-side reason to wait)
- October payrolls +165,000; August and September revised up by 84,000 combined; unemployment 4.1 percent; average hourly earnings +0.4 percent m/m, 3.1 percent y/y.
- Soft spots: involuntary part-time up 339,000; long-term unemployed 27.6 percent of the unemployed; "low hire, low fire."

Activity - pushes toward a hike
- Q3 GDP +3.4 percent annualized; real final sales to private domestic purchasers +4.9 percent. Business investment at a 1990s-boom pace.
- Softer notes: real PCE flat in September, real disposable income -0.3, saving rate 3.8 percent; Beige Book (through 5 Oct) shows only slight-to-modest growth, flat consumer spending, widespread price pressure. Housing and other rate-sensitive sectors weak.

Financial conditions - pushes toward caution on size, and is the main route to a hold
- Minutes: majority saw tightening since September - higher mortgage rates, wider spreads for AI-related and high-yield borrowers, bank stocks down sharply.
- Bowman (10 Nov) and Barr (16 Nov) describe a further drop in tech equity valuations and wider spreads over "the past couple of weeks"; the November Financial Stability Report centers on AI-related debt (over $800 billion on-balance-sheet, over $3 trillion in off-balance-sheet obligations).
- French sovereign stress noted in the minutes.

## 3. Strongest case for each outcome

Cut of 50 bp or more
- Only in a disorderly financial event before 9 December: an AI-debt unwind that hits hedge fund leverage and dealer capacity, the fire-sale channel the staff and Barr describe, severe enough to impair market functioning. Even then the Committee has said rate policy is not its first tool for financial stability, and inflation is near 3.8 percent.

Cut of 25 bp
- Same family of events at lower intensity, plus a sudden labor break in the 4 December jobs report. No participant in the pack argues for easing; the lowest 2026 dots sit at the current rate.

Hold
- The AI repricing deepens in late November, spreads gap out further, and the center decides financial conditions are doing the tightening for it. Bowman has already laid out this argument. Oil falls on a U.S.-Iran deal, letting the Committee point to a lower headline path. Warsh has said policy is "not about some fixed individual date on a calendar" and that stance should be judged by the whole path, the same reasoning that produced October's hold against 16 of 18 dots. A weak November jobs report or a soft October PCE revision would add cover.

Hike of 25 bp
- Almost all participants said further action would likely be appropriate; two already voted for it; Waller commits to at least this even in a good scenario; Barr expects more hikes; markets and the Desk survey were positioned for December. Data since October went the wrong way on inflation and the right way on jobs and growth, so the "accumulate more information" test has been answered in favor of moving. Waller's point that unpriced-out hikes would loosen conditions gives the center a reason not to disappoint. 25 bp fits the gradualism several participants endorsed and the 12-dot cluster at 4.125.

Hike of 50 bp or more
- Waller's own trigger was met by the 0.3 core CPI, and he tied 25 bp alone to a credibility risk. "A couple" of participants raised more than 25 bp in October conditional on bad inflation data, which arrived. Four dots already sat at 4.375 for year-end, reachable now only with 50. Cook argues delay forces larger moves later. If the 25 November PCE report shows core at 3.3 percent or higher with broad breadth and the 4 December payrolls are strong, the hawks can argue that 50 simply makes up for the October skip, under a chair whose slogan is "no ifs, ands, or buts."

## 4. What I am least sure of

- Warsh himself. He gives no guidance, does not submit a dot, and led a hold in October when most dots implied hiking. Inference: this raises the hold probability above what the participant-level signals alone would give, and I carry roughly 17 percent for it rather than under 10.
- How bad the November market selloff is. The pack shows it only through officials' brief references (Bowman, Barr, the FSR); there are no price levels. A mild repricing is something the Committee welcomes as transmission; a severe one is the main path to a hold and caps the 50 bp case. This widens both the hold tail and my uncertainty on size.
- Whether Waller's 50 bp marker has followers. After the CPI release, Jefferson, Barr and Cook all spoke without naming 50, and Waller's own later remarks in the pack are not about the outlook. Inference: 50 is a live minority position, not the center - this keeps hike50 in the low teens rather than above 20.
- Data still to come before the meeting: October PCE and the Q3 GDP revision (25 Nov) and November payrolls (4 Dec). November CPI lands on 10 December, after the decision, so it cannot move it.
- Geopolitics. A U.S.-Iran agreement would lower energy prices (helps hold or 25 over 50); an escalation raises both inflation and activity risks at once.
- Politics. The President publicly criticized the Board in October; the midterms are now past. Inference: a small, hard-to-size pull toward hold, already reflected in the hold weight.

Net effect: I am fairly confident in the direction (about 82 percent on some hike), less confident on the size, and I keep a meaningful hold tail because the chair's reaction function has only two meetings of tightening-era evidence.
