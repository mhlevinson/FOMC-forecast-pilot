Minutes of the Federal Open Market Committee

October 27–28, 2026
A joint meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee and the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System was held in the offices of the Board of Governors on Tuesday, October 27, 2026, at 9:30 a.m. and continued on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, at 9:00 a.m.1

Staff Review and Policymakers' Discussion of Financial Stability
The staff presented an assessment of financial stability vulnerabilities. The staff's presentation used a range of tail scenarios to illustrate how a decline in asset prices could amplify economic shocks, with investors and fund managers being forced to sell assets to deleverage and to meet redemptions, respectively. The staff noted that asset sales by return-sensitive investment vehicles such as hedge funds, bond mutual funds, and target date funds could, in very severe scenarios, be substantial enough to impair market functioning.

The staff also highlighted the rapid growth of debt associated with the artificial intelligence (AI) buildout, noting that off-balance-sheet leasing commitments and circular financing arrangements among AI-related firms could amplify losses if returns on AI investment were to disappoint.

Overall, the staff judged that current vulnerabilities could amplify shocks somewhat more than usual, with elevated asset valuations and leverage at hedge funds representing the most salient vulnerabilities, while strong bank and household balance sheets provided an important buffer against more severe outcomes.

In their discussion of financial stability, almost all participants commented on risks associated with a scenario in which the returns on AI investment turned out to be disappointing to investors. Many participants commented on the potential consequences of an AI-related asset price revaluation should it occur. Most participants observed that AI-related debt had grown rapidly and that much of this growth had taken place through complex and opaque financing structures. A few participants remarked that AI-related exposures were becoming increasingly concentrated among a small number of firms.

Several participants noted that the staff's assessment was that the financial system remained resilient, with the banking sector well capitalized and able to withstand losses under the severe scenarios considered. However, several participants cautioned that past experience suggested that banking system vulnerabilities could emerge rapidly even after seemingly reassuring initial assessments. Several participants expressed concerns that the existing data available to bank supervisors may not fully capture banks' indirect exposure through nonbank financial institutions, including credit lines to private credit funds and other complex linkages.

Most participants highlighted hedge fund leverage or limited dealer intermediation capacity as potential channels of amplification during periods of market stress. Several participants pointed to banks' prime brokerage exposures as warranting continued monitoring and supervisory attention. Some participants raised concerns about potential interactions among the level of government indebtedness, rising long-term yields, and financial stability, noting that the implications of adverse shocks for the economic outlook could be harder to gauge in a situation of diminished fiscal policy space. Some participants also highlighted cyber risk as a critical area of concern.

Almost all participants commented on potential policy responses that could address financial stability vulnerabilities. A few participants suggested that maintaining strong bank capital cushions, including through the deployment of the countercyclical capital buffer, would help ensure that commercial banks could continue intermediating credit if stress emerged elsewhere in the financial system. A few participants noted the importance of ensuring ready bank access to the discount window and other liquidity programs. Some participants highlighted potential ways of enhancing operational readiness, including those relating to the Federal Reserve's ability to lend to designated financial market utilities amid stressed conditions, with participants expressing different views on the steps to be taken, if appropriate. A few participants also discussed distinguishing Federal Reserve asset purchases undertaken for market functioning purposes from those undertaken to provide monetary policy accommodation. Several participants stressed that monetary policy was not the appropriate tool to address financial stability risks directly, though a couple of participants noted that monetary policy implementation choices could influence the nonbank sector's incentives to rely on leverage.

Developments in Financial Markets and Open Market Operations
The manager started with an overview of developments in financial markets over the intermeeting period. On net, nominal Treasury yields and inflation compensation were little changed, broad equity prices were marginally higher, and the dollar was stronger. However, asset prices exhibited notable swings over the period, as market participants absorbed economic data releases, financial disclosures by AI companies, evolving developments in the ongoing conflict with Iran, and a sharp repricing of France's sovereign debt.

With respect to Treasury securities markets, the manager noted that nominal Treasury yields had moved up significantly until early October, mostly in response to economic data that pointed to a strong economy. Subsequently, however, uncertainty about the projected revenue trajectory of key AI firms and about the sustainability of the pace of the buildout of AI data centers, together with concerns regarding French sovereign bonds, triggered renewed investor appetite for U.S. Treasury securities, winding back the previous increase in yields. The manager assessed that liquidity in the market for Treasury securities had diminished somewhat over the period but by no more than what could have been expected to occur in response to the increase in yield volatility; market functioning had continued to be orderly.

With regard to policy rate expectations, the manager observed that the federal funds rate path implied by futures prices was little changed, on net, over the period, with little chance of a rate increase associated with this meeting but high probabilities assigned to a 25 basis point hike by year-end and two more by the summer of 2027. Expectations derived from the Open Market Desk Survey of Market Expectations (Desk survey) were somewhat lower, with the median participant expecting one rate hike in December and another one in March 2027, followed by a rate cut early in 2028. The manager noted that term premiums embedded in market prices may help reconcile the differences between the two expected paths. The Desk survey's median estimate of the longer-run nominal federal funds rate increased to about 3.4 percent; an increase in estimates of the longer-run real interest rate was believed to be the source of this upward shift.

The manager then turned to developments in equity and credit markets. Equity prices rose earlier in the intermeeting period, largely driven by the AI infrastructure segment, with investors continuing to price in expectations of robust earnings growth. Risk sentiment deteriorated, however, after two leading AI firms reported revenue growth below investor expectations and a hyperscaler released capital spending plans that required a greater amount of financing than investors had anticipated. These developments weighed on hyperscalers' equity valuations, and the effects were even more pronounced on stock prices in the AI infrastructure segment. Credit spreads increased over the period in both the investment-grade and high-yield segments, with high-yield bonds issued to finance data center construction experiencing an especially notable widening in spreads. The manager assessed that, even before this intermeeting period, the higher interest rate environment was both weighing on equity prices and raising credit spreads. Higher interest rates had helped lower equity valuations and had contributed to greater investor sensitivity regarding more highly leveraged borrowers.

With regard to international developments, the manager observed that sovereign yields rose substantially in France amid heightened concerns about fiscal sustainability. The French–German 10-year yield spread surged to levels not seen since the highs reached in 2011 during the euro-area debt crisis. Market-implied policy rate paths in major advanced-economy central banks continued to indicate further policy tightening by the end of 2027. That said, whereas the U.S. policy rate path was little changed on net, market-implied policy rate paths in advanced foreign economies (AFEs) shifted down, in part reflecting perceptions of a less resilient economic outlook than in the U.S. and the AFEs' greater sensitivity to elevated energy prices. Increased investor concerns over fiscal prospects also weighed on policy rate expectations in the euro area. The widening of interest rate differentials between the AFEs and the U.S. helped drive the appreciation of the U.S. dollar.

The manager then turned to money markets. Money market conditions had remained stable over the period, with the effective federal funds rate staying 2 basis points below the interest on reserve balances (IORB) rate, and with repurchase agreement rates also averaging slightly below the IORB rate. The September corporate tax date and the September quarter-end passed uneventfully.

The manager noted that the level of reserves appeared to remain within a range consistent with an ample supply. In light of orderly money market conditions and expectations that the near-term outlook for reserve conditions would be broadly similar to the conditions observed recently, the Desk decided to keep reserve management purchases (RMPs) at zero in the current purchase period. The manager noted that the pace of RMPs was not on a preset course and that the Desk would continue to make decisions each month in pursuit of the goal of keeping the level of reserve balances within the ample range. The manager also noted that, according to the latest Senior Financial Officer Survey, reserve demand did not appear to have changed much recently, though it had increased over time, especially in the wake of the March 2023 period of banking stress.

By unanimous vote, the Committee ratified the Desk's domestic transactions over the intermeeting period. There were no intervention operations in foreign currencies for the System's account during the intermeeting period.

Staff Review of the Economic Situation
The information available at the time of the meeting indicated that inflation remained elevated. Labor market conditions continued to be broadly stable with some signs of gradual tightening. Real gross domestic product (GDP) was expanding at a solid pace.

Based on data from the consumer price index (CPI) and the producer price index, the staff estimated that price inflation—as measured by the 12-month change in the price index for total personal consumption expenditures (PCE)—rose to 3.6 percent in September, boosted by a rapid increase in consumer energy prices. Core PCE price inflation, which excludes changes in consumer energy prices and many consumer food prices, was estimated to have edged up to 3.1 percent in September. Both total and core inflation were higher than their year-earlier levels, a development that the staff attributed largely to the effects of earlier tariff increases, higher energy price inflation, and upward pressure on consumer goods prices that was associated with the AI buildout.

The unemployment rate edged up to 4.2 percent in September but remained 0.2 percentage point below its average in the second half of 2025. The labor force participation rate moved up in September and had fully retraced the decline seen since May. Nonfarm payroll gains averaged about 50,000 per month over the three months ending in September and had been reasonably broad based in recent months. Average hourly earnings rose 3 percent over the 12 months ending in September and had decelerated over the past year.

Available indicators suggested that real GDP growth picked up in the third quarter of this year following a first-half gain that had been revised up as a result of the annual update to the national accounts. Real private domestic final purchases—which comprises PCE and private fixed investment and which tends to provide a better signal of economic momentum than does real GDP—appeared to have risen strongly in the third quarter and outpaced real GDP, with solid gains in both consumer spending and business investment. The AI buildout continued to support robust increases in investment spending. Trade data for July and August showed that real goods imports continued to grow at a rapid pace, led by booming imports of high-tech products. Meanwhile, real goods exports were lower than their second-quarter average, as U.S. oil exports fell back.

Economic activity abroad continued to expand at a moderate pace, with foreign economies demonstrating resilience in the face of elevated energy prices. Manufacturing activity remained solid, supported by strong global demand for AI-related products. Labor markets were generally stable across major foreign economies.

Headline inflation remained above target levels in most foreign economies, largely reflecting rises in energy prices this year due to the Middle East conflict. In response to elevated inflation, and with real GDP growth holding up, several foreign central banks—including the Bank of Japan, the Reserve Bank of Australia, and central banks in several emerging market economies—raised their policy rates during the intermeeting period.

Staff Review of the Financial Situation
Over the intermeeting period, nominal Treasury yields were little changed, on net, and the dollar appreciated, while broad equity price indexes increased somewhat despite notable volatility late in the period tied to shifting investor sentiment about AI-related investment. Spreads on corporate debt widened, notably so for riskier corporate debt.

The expected path of the federal funds rate implied by market quotes had exhibited considerable shifts over the period since the September FOMC meeting but ended the period little changed, with market pricing assigning a high probability to no change in the target range at this meeting. Option-implied volatility of short-term interest rates increased notably, consistent with greater investor uncertainty about the policy rate path. Nominal Treasury yields rose early in the period before retracing those steps.

Broad equity price indexes rose modestly, on net, as reduced optimism regarding AI-related financing weighed on sentiment later in the period. Equity prices for interest-sensitive segments—including real estate investment trusts and smaller-capitalization firms—declined, and bank equity prices fell sharply amid concerns about slower loan growth and commercial real estate (CRE) exposures. One-month option-implied volatility on the S&P 500—the VIX—was little changed, on net, and remained at a moderate level. Spreads on investment-grade corporate bonds and benchmark municipal securities increased slightly and remained compressed by historical standards, while spreads on speculative-grade bonds widened notably, reflecting the elevated interest rate sensitivity of riskier firms.

In AFEs, sovereign bond yields exhibited significant volatility, as investors grappled with uncertainty related to the Middle East conflict and the profitability of AI-related investments. On net, long-term yields in most AFEs declined. France was a notable exception, reflecting heightened market concerns about its fiscal trajectory. The broad dollar appreciated, reflecting the widening of U.S.–AFE yield differentials and depreciation pressures on some Latin American currencies.

Financing conditions in domestic credit markets remained broadly supportive of economic activity even as borrowing costs rose notably over the intermeeting period across a range of markets. Conditions in short-term funding markets continued to be stable, with the September increase in the target range for the federal funds rate passing through promptly into overnight funding markets.

Credit remained broadly available through September, though private credit remained constrained and residential mortgage activity declined further amid the sharp rise in mortgage rates. Gross issuance of investment-grade corporate bonds remained solid, while speculative-grade gross issuance picked up to a strong pace, likely reflecting mounting pressure on riskier firms to refinance. Bank lending standards were unchanged in the October Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey on Bank Lending Practices at levels estimated to be slightly easier than historical medians. Mortgage credit availability continued to ease gradually, even as elevated interest rates kept origination activity subdued. Consumer credit remained generally available, with credit card and auto lending standards being little changed to somewhat easier.

Credit performance remained solid in most markets, though forward-looking measures deteriorated somewhat, consistent with potential challenges associated with elevated interest rates, particularly for leveraged and rate-sensitive businesses. Corporate bond defaults remained low, while leveraged loan default rates continued to be somewhat elevated, reflecting continued use of distressed exchanges; the share of distressed private credit loans, particularly among software firms, increased in the second quarter. Delinquency rates for commercial mortgage-backed securities remained elevated. Household credit performance remained sound on balance, though credit card delinquencies, concentrated among a limited set of consistent revolving accounts, stayed elevated relative to pre-pandemic norms.

Staff Economic Outlook
The staff's inflation forecast was similar to the one prepared for the September meeting. Total inflation was expected to step down sharply next year as energy prices decelerated, while the boost to inflation from tariff increases, geopolitical turmoil, and the AI buildout was expected to mostly fade over the next couple of years. Inflation was expected to be about 2 percent in 2028.

The staff's outlook for economic activity in 2026 was a little stronger relative to the forecast prepared for the September meeting, mostly in response to incoming data. Thereafter, the outlook was similar to the September forecast: Real GDP was expected to slightly outpace potential through 2028, with spending supported by AI-related investment and financial conditions. The unemployment rate was expected to remain below the staff's estimate of its longer-run rate through 2029.

The staff continued to view the uncertainty around the projection as substantial, with the outlook clouded by risks associated with persistently high inflation, the pace of AI-related investment, possible productivity gains from AI adoption, and geopolitical developments. Risks to the inflation projection were seen as skewed to the upside: In addition to the possibility that the AI buildout and geopolitical turmoil might put greater-than-expected upward pressure on prices, the staff viewed the possibility that longer-run inflation expectations could have become unanchored after five years' worth of elevated inflation as a salient risk. In contrast, the risks around the forecasts for employment and real GDP growth were seen as more balanced.

Policymakers' Discussion of Current Conditions, the Economic Outlook, and Monetary Policy Considerations
Participants acknowledged that inflation remained elevated. Several participants noted that the annual revision to the national accounts had led to a reduction in measured inflation for this year, though a couple emphasized that the revision did not indicate an improvement in the basic inflation outlook. Several participants observed that tariff-related effects on prices appeared to be waning; however, most remarked that inflation pressures in the economy were broad based. The vast majority noted that high energy prices continued to contribute to current inflation pressures. Many participants observed that demand for products associated with the AI buildout was continuing to strain supply and raise prices of technology-related consumer goods, and several judged that price pressures were increasingly extending beyond industries directly affected by data center construction. A number of participants also pointed to elevated inflation in the category of market-based core nonhousing services and to measures of underlying inflation that remained above 2 percent.

Participants assessed that medium- and longer-term inflation expectations continued to be consistent with the Committee's 2 percent objective. A few, however, noted that market- and survey-based measures of shorter-term inflation expectations had increased or cautioned that market-based measures of inflation expectations—being derived from the pricing of securities indexed to the CPI—might be difficult to interpret in the current circumstance of an unusual discrepancy between CPI and PCE inflation rates. Some participants expressed concern that more widespread price pressures in the context of a prolonged period of above-target inflation could increase the risk of elevated inflation becoming entrenched in the inflation expectations of households and businesses.

Most participants expected inflation to remain elevated in the near term but to decline over time toward 2 percent, reflecting the waning of tariff-related price effects, a reduction in energy prices' contribution to inflation, and a moderation of the pressures associated with the AI buildout. The vast majority saw upside risks to this outlook, citing higher energy prices and continued supply constraints with regard to refined fuel products. Many participants viewed the AI buildout as a material source of inflation risk. Some judged that the current investment surge could raise aggregate demand relative to supply if capacity expansion proved slower than expected. A majority noted the possibility that higher costs could be passed through more broadly and persistently to consumer and business prices. A few, however, observed that price pressures could diminish as productive capacity and productivity increased. A number of participants remarked that greater scrutiny of the sector's returns and financing could lead to a slowing in AI-related spending, thereby tending to reduce inflation pressures.

Participants characterized labor market conditions as stable and close to maximum employment, citing an unemployment rate near estimates of its natural rate, payroll gains within the range of estimates of breakeven employment growth, and low levels of layoffs and initial claims for unemployment insurance. Many pointed to signs of modest tightening in the labor market, citing the decline in the unemployment rate since late 2025. At the same time, some noted that labor market dynamism remained unusually low, as reflected in low rates of hiring and layoffs, a low job-finding rate, elevated long-term unemployment, and perceptions of weak job availability. Most judged that aggregate nominal wage growth remained moderate and was broadly consistent with the Committee's 2 percent inflation objective. Many observed that shortages of skilled workers needed for data center construction and related industries had contributed to sizable wage premiums in those industries, while a few noted that reduced labor supply in industries reliant on immigrant workers could add to sectoral wage pressures, although these developments did not appear to be having a material effect on aggregate wage measures. Participants generally expected labor market conditions to remain stable in the near term and viewed upside and downside risks to the labor market as broadly balanced.

Several participants indicated that financial conditions overall remained supportive of continued economic growth, citing elevated equity valuations and broadly available credit. However, a majority of participants noted a tightening in financial conditions since the September meeting, citing higher mortgage interest rates as well as wider credit spreads for some higher-risk and AI-related borrowers and reduced issuance, particularly by lower-rated firms. Many observed that higher borrowing costs and more selective lending had weighed somewhat on residential investment, CRE, small business borrowing, and some other interest-sensitive sectors. Many pointed to lower AI-related equity valuations amid apparent reassessments of prospective returns and financing risks and judged that greater market discipline over highly leveraged projects could temper investment demand.

Participants generally assessed that economic activity continued to expand at a strong pace, supported by resilient household spending and robust business fixed investment. A couple of participants noted that revisions to the national accounts had strengthened the view that households had sufficient income and savings to sustain their consumption spending. A number of participants cited elevated household net worth and strong corporate earnings as factors supporting household spending and business investment, while several noted that productivity gains were a sign of the economy's resilience. At the same time, a number of participants observed that growth remained uneven, with AI-related investment, manufacturing, and data center construction being particularly strong, whereas housing and other interest-sensitive sectors were weak. A few noted that higher prices of necessities were straining the budgets of lower- and middle-income households, while increased energy and borrowing costs were leading some firms to reconsider production plans and the pace of expansion. A couple of participants observed that conditions in the agricultural sector had been strained by higher prices of diesel and other inputs.

Some participants discussed the implications of the AI-related buildout for productivity and potential output. These participants judged that stronger productivity would allow output to expand without correspondingly tightening the labor market. At the same time, a number of participants emphasized considerable uncertainty regarding the timing, magnitude, and distribution of AI-related productivity gains. Several participants also noted risks that the AI investment surge could be excessive, leading to lower-than-expected returns, and a couple of participants noted that it could be crowding out other types of investment.

A number of participants shared their views on monetary policy strategy. They stressed the importance of restoring inflation to 2 percent on a sustained basis, preventing persistent relative-price shocks from giving rise to broad inflation pressures, and maintaining well-anchored inflation expectations. They also emphasized the importance of adjusting the stance of monetary policy in response to incoming information and its implications for the economic outlook amid uncertainty about the neutral rate, the interest sensitivity of AI-related demand, and financial conditions, and risks to the Committee's dual-mandate objectives.

In their consideration of monetary policy at this meeting, most participants expressed support for maintaining the target range for the federal funds rate. Many of these participants pointed to benefits in accumulating additional information, which would help in determining the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2 percent goal. These participants cited the need to assess the durability of AI-related investment demand, the evolution of financing conditions, the transmission of the September rate increase to the economy, or geopolitical developments. A couple of participants noted that, at the current juncture, a gradual approach to policy firming would give businesses and households time to adjust to higher interest rates. Several participants emphasized that monetary policy's stance and the effects of that stance should be evaluated by considering the overall path of the federal funds rate over time rather than actions taken at individual meetings. Some participants observed that market-implied expectations of tightening steps in December and next year were broadly consistent with their assessments of the appropriate policy rate path, and several saw this alignment as reducing the risks associated with keeping the target range unchanged at this meeting. A few participants, while supporting maintaining the current target range for the federal funds rate, indicated that they viewed the decision as a close call. Many participants stressed the importance of clearly relaying the message that the decision to maintain the target range did not reflect a weakening of the Committee's commitment to returning inflation to 2 percent in a timely manner.

Some participants favored an increase of 25 basis points in the target range at this meeting, emphasizing that inflation had remained too high and that they saw few signs of it abating. They judged that the current policy stance was not sufficiently restrictive, and a few of them warned that further delay risked allowing high inflation to become entrenched or necessitating a larger and more disruptive tightening later. A few participants observed that only a limited amount of additional data would become available in coming weeks, geopolitical uncertainty was unlikely to be substantially resolved in the near future, or the next inflation release was likely to be unfavorable. A few participants who favored an increase in the target range indicated that they could have supported no change in the federal funds rate provided that it was clearly understood by market participants that further tightening was likely.

With regard to the outlook for monetary policy in the months ahead, almost all participants judged that, under their baseline outlooks for inflation and the labor market, further action to raise the target range for the federal funds rate—broadly consistent with the September Summary of Economic Projections—would likely be appropriate, in order to ensure a timely return of inflation to the 2 percent objective. A couple of participants noted that an increase of more than 25 basis points could warrant consideration at the next meeting if incoming inflation data were unfavorable. A few participants observed that inflation could prove more persistent than anticipated, in which case a shift to a more restrictive policy stance could become necessary. Participants remarked that future policy decisions would depend on the incoming data, the evolving outlook, and the balance of risks.

Committee Policy Actions
In support of the Committee's dual-mandate goals, 10 members agreed to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent and reaffirmed the FOMC's policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking system. Members agreed that the unemployment rate was largely unchanged and that economic activity was expanding at a strong pace amid resilient domestic spending, while inflation remained elevated relative to the Committee's 2 percent goal. Members noted that the accumulation of more information would help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2 percent goal. The Committee underlined its continuing resolve to achieve its dual-mandate goals by indicating in its postmeeting statement that it "will deliver price stability." Two members voted against the decision to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate, preferring an increase of 25 basis points in the target range at this meeting.

At the conclusion of the discussion, the Committee voted to direct the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, until instructed otherwise, to execute transactions in the System Open Market Account in accordance with the following domestic policy directive, for release at 2:00 p.m.:

"Effective October 29, 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee directs the Desk to:

Undertake open market operations as necessary to maintain the federal funds rate in a target range of 3-3/4 to 4 percent.
Conduct standing overnight repurchase agreement operations at a rate of 4.0 percent.
Conduct standing overnight reverse repurchase agreement operations at an offering rate of 3.75 percent and with a per-counterparty limit of $160 billion per day.
When appropriate, increase the System Open Market Account holdings of securities through purchases of Treasury bills and, if needed, other Treasury securities with remaining maturities of 3 years or less to maintain an ample level of reserves.
Roll over at auction all principal payments from the Federal Reserve's holdings of Treasury securities. Reinvest all principal payments from the Federal Reserve's holdings of agency securities into Treasury bills."

The vote also encompassed approval of the statement below for release at 2:00 p.m.:

"The Federal Open Market Committee approved the following statement for release by a 10 — 2 vote:

The Committee decided to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent, in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate. The Committee is continuing its policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking system.

Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace. While uncertainty remains elevated owing, in part, to geopolitical developments, domestic spending has been resilient. Productivity growth is strong, and capital investment is robust. Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little.

Inflation remains elevated. The accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2 percent goal. The Committee will deliver price stability."

Voting for this action: Kevin Warsh, John C. Williams, Michael S. Barr, Michelle W. Bowman, Philip N. Jefferson, Neel Kashkari, Lorie K. Logan, Anna Paulson, Jerome H. Powell, and Christopher J. Waller.

Voting against this action: Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack, who preferred to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point at this meeting.

Consistent with the Committee's decision to leave the target range for the federal funds rate unchanged, the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System voted unanimously to maintain the interest rate paid on reserve balances to 3.90 percent, effective October 29, 2026. The Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System voted unanimously to approve the establishment of the primary credit rate at the existing level of 4.0 percent.

It was agreed that the next meeting of the Committee would be held on Tuesday–Wednesday, December 8–9, 2026. The meeting adjourned at 10:30 a.m. on October 28, 2026.

Notation Vote
By notation vote completed on October 6, 2026, the Committee unanimously approved the minutes of the Committee meeting held on September 15–16, 2026.

Federal Reserve issues FOMC statement

For release at 2:00 p.m. EDT

Share

The Federal Open Market Committee approved the following statement for release by a 10 – 2 vote:

The Committee decided to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent, in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate. The Committee is continuing its policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking system.

Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace. While uncertainty remains elevated owing, in part, to geopolitical developments, domestic spending has been resilient. Productivity growth is strong, and capital investment is robust. Job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little.

Inflation remains elevated. The accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2 percent goal. The Committee will deliver price stability.

 

Voting against the monetary policy action were Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack, who preferred to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point at this meeting.

Implementation Note issued October 28, 2026

Decisions Regarding Monetary Policy Implementation

The Federal Reserve has made the following decisions to implement the monetary policy stance announced by the Federal Open Market Committee in its statement on October 28, 2026:

The Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System voted unanimously to maintain the interest rate paid on reserve balances at 3.90 percent, effective October 29, 2026.

As part of its policy decision, the Federal Open Market Committee voted to direct the Open Market Desk at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, until instructed otherwise, to execute transactions in the System Open Market Account in accordance with the following domestic policy directive:

"Effective October 29, 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee directs the Desk to:

Undertake open market operations as necessary to maintain the federal funds rate in a target range of 3-3/4 to 4 percent.
Conduct standing overnight repurchase agreement operations at a rate of 4.0 percent.
Conduct standing overnight reverse repurchase agreement operations at an offering rate of 3.75 percent and with a per-counterparty limit of $160 billion per day.
When appropriate, increase the System Open Market Account holdings of securities through purchases of Treasury bills and, if needed, other Treasury securities with remaining maturities of 3 years or less to maintain an ample level of reserves.
Roll over at auction all principal payments from the Federal Reserve's holdings of Treasury securities. Reinvest all principal payments from the Federal Reserve's holdings of agency securities into Treasury bills."

In a related action, the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System voted unanimously to approve the establishment of the primary credit rate at the existing level of 4.0 percent.

This information will be updated as appropriate to reflect decisions of the Federal Open Market Committee or the Board of Governors regarding details of the Federal Reserve's operational tools and approach used to implement monetary policy.

More information regarding open market operations and reinvestments may be found on the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's website.

Summary of Economic Projections

In conjunction with the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting held on September 15–16, 2026, meeting participants submitted their projections of the most likely outcomes for real gross domestic product (GDP) growth, the unemployment rate, and inflation for each year from 2026 to 2029 and over the longer run. Each participant's projections were based on information available at the time of the meeting, together with her or his assessment of appropriate monetary policy—including a path for the federal funds rate and its longer-run value—and assumptions about other factors likely to affect economic outcomes. The longer-run projections represent each participant's assessment of the value to which each variable would be expected to converge, over time, under appropriate monetary policy and in the absence of further shocks to the economy. "Appropriate monetary policy" is defined as the future path of policy that each participant deems most likely to foster outcomes for economic activity and inflation that best satisfy his or her individual interpretation of the statutory mandate to promote maximum employment and price stability.

Forecast Uncertainty

The economic projections provided by the members of the Board of Governors and the presidents of the Federal Reserve Banks inform discussions of monetary policy among policymakers and can aid public understanding of the basis for policy actions. Considerable uncertainty attends these projections, however. The economic and statistical models and relationships used to help produce economic forecasts are necessarily imperfect descriptions of the real world, and the future path of the economy can be affected by myriad unforeseen developments and events. Thus, in setting the stance of monetary policy, participants consider not only what appears to be the most likely economic outcome as embodied in their projections, but also the range of alternative possibilities, the likelihood of their occurring, and the potential costs to the economy should they occur.

Table 2 summarizes the average historical accuracy of a range of forecasts, including those reported in past Monetary Policy Reports and those prepared by the Federal Reserve Board's staff in advance of meetings of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC). The projection error ranges shown in the table illustrate the considerable uncertainty associated with economic forecasts. For example, suppose a participant projects that real gross domestic product (GDP) and total consumer prices will rise steadily at annual rates of, respectively, 3 percent and 2 percent. If the uncertainty attending those projections is similar to that experienced in the past and the risks around the projections are broadly balanced, the numbers reported in table 2 would imply a probability of about 70 percent that actual GDP would expand within a range of 1.6 to 4.4 percent in the current year, 1.2 to 4.8 percent in the second year, 0.9 to 5.1 percent in the third year, and 0.7 to 5.3 percent in the fourth year. The corresponding 70 percent confidence intervals for overall inflation would be 1.0 to 3.0 percent in the current year, 0.3 to 3.7 percent in the second year, 0.2 to 3.8 percent in the third year, and 0.3 to 3.7 percent in the fourth year. Figures 4.A through 4.C illustrate these confidence bounds in "fan charts" that are symmetric and centered on the medians of FOMC participants' projections for GDP growth, the unemployment rate, and inflation. However, in some instances, the risks around the projections may not be symmetric. In particular, the unemployment rate cannot be negative; furthermore, the risks around a particular projection might be tilted to either the upside or the downside, in which case the corresponding fan chart would be asymmetrically positioned around the median projection.

Because current conditions may differ from those that prevailed, on average, over history, participants provide judgments as to whether the uncertainty attached to their projections of each economic variable is greater than, smaller than, or broadly similar to typical levels of forecast uncertainty seen in the past 20 years, as presented in table 2 and reflected in the widths of the confidence intervals shown in the top panels of figures 4.A through 4.C. Participants' current assessments of the uncertainty surrounding their projections are summarized in the bottom-left panels of those figures. Participants also provide judgments as to whether the risks to their projections are weighted to the upside, are weighted to the downside, or are broadly balanced. That is, while the symmetric historical fan charts shown in the top panels of figures 4.A through 4.C imply that the risks to participants' projections are balanced, participants may judge that there is a greater risk that a given variable will be above rather than below their projections. These judgments are summarized in the lower-right panels of figures 4.A through 4.C.

As with real activity and inflation, the outlook for the future path of the federal funds rate is subject to considerable uncertainty. This uncertainty arises primarily because each participant's assessment of the appropriate stance of monetary policy depends importantly on the evolution of real activity and inflation over time. If economic conditions evolve in an unexpected manner, then assessments of the appropriate setting of the federal funds rate would change from that point forward. The final line in table 2 shows the error ranges for forecasts of short-term interest rates. They suggest that the historical confidence intervals associated with projections of the federal funds rate are quite wide. It should be noted, however, that these confidence intervals are not strictly consistent with the projections for the federal funds rate, as these projections are not forecasts of the most likely quarterly outcomes but rather are projections of participants' individual assessments of appropriate monetary policy and are on an end-of-year basis. However, the forecast errors should provide a sense of the uncertainty around the future path of the federal funds rate generated by the uncertainty about the macroeconomic variables as well as additional adjustments to monetary policy that would be appropriate to offset the effects of shocks to the economy.

If at some point in the future the confidence interval around the federal funds rate were to extend below zero, it would be truncated at zero for purposes of the fan chart shown in figure 5; zero is the bottom of the lowest target range for the federal funds rate that has been adopted by the Committee in the past. This approach to the construction of the federal funds rate fan chart would be merely a convention; it would not have any implications for possible future policy decisions regarding the use of negative interest rates to provide additional monetary policy accommodation if doing so were appropriate. In such situations, the Committee could also employ other tools, including forward guidance and asset purchases, to provide additional accommodation.

While figures 4.A through 4.C provide information on the uncertainty around the economic projections, figure 1 provides information on the range of views across FOMC participants. A comparison of figure 1 with figures 4.A through 4.C shows that the dispersion of the projections across participants is much smaller than the average forecast errors over the past 20 years.

November 20, 2026

The U.S. Economy in Global Context

Governor Lisa D. Cook

At the XXIX Annual Conference of the Central Bank of Chile, Santiago, Chile

Share
Watch Live

Thank you, Governor Costa, for that kind introduction and for the invitation to deliver this keynote address and to participate in the 29th Annual Conference of the Central Bank of Chile today.1 It is a distinct privilege to be back in Santiago. I first came to Chile between my second and third years as a Ph.D. student at Berkeley after completing a summer as an intern at the World Bank. I was on a quest to determine whether economic growth in Latin America or in another region of the world would be the subject of my dissertation. It piqued my interest in inflation targeting, a sound fiscal framework, and now, of course, how to think about the economic forces reshaping the global economy. I am grateful for the chance to continue to learn from your experience and to share some of my own thinking.

I want to begin by acknowledging a fact that may seem obvious but is easy to overlook in the day-to-day work of setting monetary policy: The U.S. economy does not sit in isolation. As a member of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors, my mandate is domestic—to promote maximum employment and price stability in the United States. But achieving that mandate requires that I pay careful attention to global developments, including economic and financial developments in Chile and across Latin America. The forces reconfiguring your economies are, in many cases, identical to the forces reconfiguring ours, even if they arrive on our respective shores somewhat differently.

Geopolitics and Trade
Let me start with geopolitical events. Over the past several years, the global economy has absorbed a remarkable sequence of shocks. Russia's war in Ukraine disrupted energy and commodity markets worldwide. The conflict in the Middle East pushed up energy prices again this year, and experts have warned that damaged infrastructure and depleted inventories could keep oil prices elevated for an extended period. These are not simply North American concerns. Oil is a globally traded commodity, and a conflict that drives up the price of oil in Houston drives it up in Santiago, as well. The precise transmission differs across countries, depending on whether they are net energy importers or exporters, but few economies have been insulated.

A related story applies to trade. Over the past two years, the U.S. has raised tariffs on a wide range of imports, and considerable uncertainty remains about where tariff levels will ultimately settle. In the U.S., the effects of tariffs on inflation appear to have moderated, even as uncertainty about future trade policy persists. China, too, has increased trade restrictions, limiting exports of critical minerals for which it supplies a large fraction of the world's production. These minerals are a crucial input, and uncertainty is rising around the world about access to them. I raise this not to make a judgment about trade policy—that is not my role—but because such shifts inevitably reverberate across the global economy. When trade relationships between major economies are altered, other countries must decide how to respond: whether to deepen commercial ties elsewhere, reconfigure supply chains, or wait for greater clarity before committing to new investment. These are decisions playing out in boardrooms and finance ministries around the world right now, including, I suspect, in some of the economies represented in this room.

The Global AI Buildout
The third global force I want to highlight—and the one I will spend the most time on—is the extraordinary buildout of artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure now underway around the world.

While at the Board, I have spent much of my career as an economist studying innovation, including AI. Now, I spend considerable time talking with contacts in the U.S. and around the world about how AI is evolving and being used. I have spoken on several occasions about how I view AI—as a general-purpose technology, on par with or perhaps exceeding the steam engine, electrification, and the internet in its potential to transform how we work, innovate, and grow. This technology could bring a productivity boom unparalleled in generations.2 At the same time, I worry deeply about our shared risks resulting from the technology, such as the specter of a painful labor market transition or cyber and financial-stability risks. Uncertainty abounds.

In the U.S., AI has catalyzed a historic wave of investment. Companies have announced plans for more than $2 trillion in specific data center construction projects. So far, only a fraction of this has been realized.3 This investment is already showing up in output data. And there is evidence of spillovers to other parts of the economy—in the price of chips and high-tech equipment, construction wages, and electricity costs.

What I want to stress today is that this is not simply a North American phenomenon. By some estimates, roughly one-half of the world's data-center capacity now under construction is being built outside the U.S.4 The AI buildout is a global undertaking, and it is reshaping economies—including economies in this region—in ways that are only beginning to be understood.

Chile and its neighbors sit at an interesting intersection of this story. This country is the world's largest producer of copper and the second-largest producer of lithium—two minerals increasingly central to the infrastructure that AI requires, including power transmission and energy-storage systems.5 This raises some questions to which I do not think any of us has a full answer. As demand for these minerals rises and falls with the pace of the global AI buildout, does this represent a new source of growth for resource-rich economies or a new source of volatility layered on top of already volatile commodity cycles? As capital flows toward AI infrastructure and related resource extraction around the world, what are the implications for smaller open economies managing capital flows, exchange rates, and financial stability?

Corresponding to the buildout in physical infrastructure, adoption of AI tools by businesses and individuals is spreading quickly as well, albeit unevenly across countries. Recent work tracking the share of the working-age population using generative AI tools finds diffusion ranging from roughly 25 to 45 percent across higher-income economies, including Chile. These figures already exceed the pace of adoption we saw for personal computers at a comparable stage.6 This matters a great deal for growth, because wider adoption of a general-purpose technology is ultimately how its productivity gains would be realized throughout an economy—gains that, in principle, could be sizeable.

Simultaneously, researchers have raised questions about the distribution of gains across countries. Recent analysis from the International Monetary Fund suggests that advanced economies may be positioned to capture a disproportionate share of global AI-driven productivity growth over the next decade, relative to developing economies.7 If this pattern holds, it raises important questions: Where in the distribution will countries like Chile wind up, and what might determine distributional outcomes?

Finally, on the AI buildout, I would like to address AI and the labor market, the focus of today's conference. As with any major new technology, AI is likely to cause a shift in the labor market with jobs and job tasks being replaced by AI, and new jobs—many of which we cannot yet conceive of—being created. Ultimately, history has shown that human innovation will likely lead to a successful transition, with permanent job loss unlikely. However, I am very aware of the nonzero probability that this transition could take time and that job losses due to AI could precede new job creation.

In the U.S., labor markets appear resilient to date, but AI adoption is still in its early stages, and I think it is too soon to feel comforted. We do see that some workers have already been affected by AI. For instance, there is already some evidence that labor demand has cooled for coding jobs in the software industry and in simultaneous translation. In addition, younger adults are experiencing prolonged job searches, which some attribute to nascent pressure from AI on entry-level positions. As AI adoption spreads, younger workers may continue to be disproportionately affected, and firms may need to take innovative and creative steps to train their future workforce.

I am watching carefully the speed and acceleration of AI adoption across sectors and noting evidence of the changing mix of jobs within firms. New types of tasks and jobs are already being created, and I remain hopeful that job creation will be able to keep pace with job destruction.

I will now turn to my outlook on the U.S. economy.

U.S. Economic Outlook
Overall economic growth in the U.S. is strong—and accelerating. The labor market remains solid, and, of great concern to me, inflation is well above the Fed's 2 percent target and has been for 67 months. I will start my forecast, as I typically do, with the price-stability side of our dual mandate, because, given the current state of the economy, this is my focus. Then, I will give my views on the path of monetary policy. One theme I want to emphasize is that waiting to act when a policy adjustment is required has a cost.

Inflation
Inflation, as measured by the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, has exceeded the 2 percent target described in our framework for nearly six years. Based on U.S. inflation data released last week, it is estimated that the 12-month change in the PCE price index was 3.8 percent in October. The 12-month change in core prices—which exclude volatile food and energy costs—was 3.2 percent in October. The core measure underscores that, while energy is a significant contributor to inflation, the sources of price pressure facing American families are increasingly varied.

Inflation soared in 2021 and 2022 because of supply disruptions and surging demand following the reopening of the U.S. and economies around the world as the global pandemic abated. The Fed, much like other central banks, responded by aggressively raising interest rates to fight inflation. I supported those actions as soon as I joined the Fed. From the middle of 2022 until the early part of last year, inflation moved down and appeared to be on a sustainable path back toward the 2 percent objective. However, a series of economic shocks over the past 18 months—including significant changes to trade policy, geopolitical developments, and massive investment in technological infrastructure—partially reversed that progress. Annual inflation in the U.S. has been steadily above 3 percent for much of this year, and, thus far, there is little evidence inflation is declining. In fact, the most recent inflation release showed that prices are rising briskly in a very broad range of categories—including airfare, appliances, furniture, hotels, and restaurants, to name a few.

Labor market and output
Next, I will turn to the labor market, the other half of the dual mandate assigned to the Fed by Congress. The U.S. labor market is solid, with still-low unemployment and consistent, though modest, hiring. The unemployment rate was 4.1 percent in October and has trended down modestly for nearly a year. As I have already mentioned, there is little evidence that AI is producing a significant imprint on the aggregate unemployment rate. Other data indicate the labor market is roughly in balance and gradually improving. Payrolls climbed by 165,000 in October, and some evidence is emerging that hiring is broadening to a wider array of sectors. Job openings in September were largely unchanged at 7.5 million, and initial unemployment claims, a proxy for layoffs, remain near historical lows—at a four-week moving average of about 195,000.

Despite signs of strength, some Americans remain understandably frustrated with the state of the labor market. The "low hire" environment is particularly challenging for new entrants. And wages are growing but not enough to overcome the damaging effects of inflation. Still, low unemployment is consistent with achieving our maximum-employment mandate, and, therefore, as a monetary policymaker, I am more focused on the inflation side of our remit.

Economic output in the U.S. has accelerated. GDP rose at a 3.4 percent annual pace in the third quarter, a pickup from already solid growth in the first half. The AI infrastructure buildout I previously discussed is a significant contributor to this acceleration. At the same time, American consumers have proven to be resilient, with real household spending rising an annualized 3.8 percent last quarter despite higher energy prices and broader inflationary pressure.

Monetary Policy
This leads to the path of U.S. monetary policy. The Federal Open Market Committee in September raised the target range for the federal funds rate to 3-3/4 to 4 percent to support a timelier return to the 2 percent objective. I supported that decision. At the most recent meeting, in October, the Committee voted to hold the policy rate steady. I dissented from that decision. I preferred to raise the target range another 1/4 percentage point. Two main reasons informed my decision to dissent.

First, in my view, financial conditions are too accommodative at a time when inflation remains stubbornly above target, with no clear signs of disinflation in train. The current stance of policy does not appear to be curtailing the AI buildout, and equity prices continue to support consumer spending. As a result, holding our policy rate steady will lead to a positive and widening output gap and increasing inflationary pressure. Indeed, we are seeing elevated inflation in a broad swath of goods and services. I see the act of holding the rate steady as much a monetary policy action as the act of increasing the rate.

Second, my vote reflected the urgency I feel to tackle inflation. Inflation has been above target for more than five and a half years, and every additional day poses greater risk that price- and wage-setters will adjust their inflation expectations upward. Furthermore, waiting longer to implement rate increases risks getting behind the curve. If we fall behind, additional and larger rate increases may be required later to catch up, which could increase stress on our financial system and have unnecessarily damaging effects on American households.

I have previously discussed my growing concern about the direction of inflation in several speeches this year.8 I have called inflation unacceptably high. This remains my view. I have articulated my frustration that inflation has moved in the wrong direction as a series of shocks stacked upon each other. In addition, I noted that the extended time inflation had exceeded our target was an important part of my calculation. In a different environment, the Committee could afford to be patient, but I continue to believe that in this economic climate we do not have that luxury. I am skeptical that September's single 1/4 point rate increase will be enough to change the path of inflation. The number and magnitude of future adjustments should be informed by observations of the economy's reaction to our policy actions. The inflation data received since the September rate increase, including reports last week, fail to provide me with sufficient evidence that inflation is returning to a sustainable path.

Conclusion
Allow me to close where I began. The forces I have described today—geopolitical conflict, shifting trade relationships, and the buildout of AI—extend beyond national borders and weigh on the minds of monetary policymakers, including those in the U.S. and here in Chile. In terms of my view of U.S. policy, there is an overriding issue we must confront: Inflation has been above our target for far too long. I take great encouragement from the resilience of the labor market and the American economy—and the promise of the technologies now reshaping it. This promise will be best supported if we restore stable prices on which sustainable growth depends. Delivering on both sides of the dual mandate is the task before the Federal Reserve, and I am committed to seeing it through.

Thank you for your time today, and thank you for the warm welcome back to Santiago.

November 20, 2026

Opening Remarks

Governor Christopher J. Waller

At the 2026 College Fed Challenge Finals, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, Washington, D.C.

Share

Thank you, Robin, and welcome, students and teachers, to the Federal Reserve Board.1

We are gathered for the finals of the annual College Fed Challenge, a tradition begun 23 years ago under Chairman Alan Greenspan. The challenge is to prepare and present a monetary policy recommendation that will best promote the Federal Reserve's dual mandate of stable prices and maximum sustainable employment. Students on the seven teams that will compete today represent the University of California, Los Angeles; the University of Chicago; Cleveland State University; Harvard College; the University of Maryland, College Park; Pennsylvania State University; and St. Lawrence University.

Let me start by congratulating all of the students here today, and your faculty advisers, for advancing to the finals by winning preliminary rounds of the competition. A record 141 schools participated this year, from 34 states. It is incredibly gratifying to think of the number of young people motivated to learn about the Federal Reserve's role in promoting a healthy economy.

A few weeks from now, my colleagues on the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) and I will be sitting at this same table, facing the same challenge you face today. Like you, we will be examining publicly available data on economic and financial conditions and will apply our understanding of how monetary policy influences those conditions to render our best judgment of the policy stance that best furthers the Fed's goals.

Crucial to that decision will be clearly explaining the Committee's actions and intentions to the public—the same task that you will face later this morning, when you present your recommendations to our judges. As I hope you have learned, clear communication is essential because monetary policy works to a considerable extent by influencing the public's view of current and future economic and financial conditions.

Monetary policy has evolved since this competition began in 2003. At that time, the FOMC relied on a single tool to influence financial conditions and the broader economy—it set a target for the federal funds rate. In the years since, the Committee expanded its toolkit with unconventional policy tools.

In an important way, the competition you will engage in today is very much like what the FOMC does at its meetings. On December 8, my 18 FOMC colleagues and I will each present our view of the economic outlook, and on December 9, our view of the proper stance of monetary policy. Before we express those views to the public, we communicate with each other, presenting evidence, testing hypotheses, and making arguments that will ultimately help us arrive at a consensus that is summarized in our postmeeting statement. Every monetary policy speech I give has its origins in this room, in the argument I make here, and the extent to which that argument holds up to what I hear from my colleagues. When I engage in the essential task of explaining monetary policy to the public, I am really doing the same thing that I do here at FOMC meetings. And very much what you will do today. Present the evidence. Make an argument. Persuade.

I hope your preparations for this event have added to your understanding of the economy and the Federal Reserve's role and perhaps will even spur some of you to pursue further study of economics and finance. Among the talented individuals who are participating in today's challenge, I hope there are some who may one day contribute to our understanding of the economy and the work of the Fed. But I hope that all of you have benefited from this experience by adding to your knowledge and developing research, analytical, and communication skills that will help you in whatever careers you choose.

I'd like to thank the judges who have given their time and offered their expertise and experience to the evaluation of each team's work. They represent the very best of the Federal Reserve. From the Board we have Sriya Anbil, who is an economist in the Board's Division of Monetary Affairs; Karen Pence, who is a deputy director in the Division of Research and Statistics; and Brian Doyle, who is a deputy director in the Division of International Finance. And from the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas we have Sam Schulhofer-Wohl, a senior vice president and senior adviser to president Lorie Logan.

Also, let me again thank the faculty advisers of each of the seven teams for helping guide, motivate, and prepare today's finalists. And thanks also to the Federal Reserve's economic education staff who have organized the Fed Challenge this year and to all the judges who volunteered their time to make it possible.

I am looking forward to a spirited competition, and I wish you all the best of luck.

Monetary Policy amid Frequent and Complex Shocks

Vice Chair Philip N. Jefferson

At the International Monetary Fund and Central Bank of Brazil Conference “A Quarter Century of Inflation Targeting in Latin America: Progress, Challenges, and the Road Ahead,” Brasília, Brazil

Share
Watch Live

Thank you, Nigel, for that kind introduction, and thank you to the Central Bank of Brazil and International Monetary Fund for hosting this important conference.1 It is an honor to be here with my fellow panelists. Today, I will briefly discuss issues that are critical for central bankers around the world—that is, how to assess and confront economic shocks. I discussed these issues in more depth in a speech earlier this year at Stanford University.2

Shocks, by their nature, are difficult to predict and vary in length, but we are nonetheless very familiar with them. Just in the past few years, we have experienced a pandemic, a rapid rise in energy prices associated with geopolitical tensions, changes to trade policy, and a technological breakthrough. One way to classify shocks is to determine whether their initial effect is on the demand side or the supply side of the economy. Demand shocks affect spending by households, businesses, or the government without changing productive capacity. Supply shocks change productive capacity—what economists call "potential output"—by affecting the available supply of labor and capital or the productivity of those inputs. The nature and duration of these shocks influence the approach to monetary policymaking.

As a monetary policymaker in the United States, I must consider whether and how shocks impact both sides of our dual mandate—maximum employment and stable prices. When a shock moves the output gap and inflation in the same direction, our objectives are aligned, and policy actions taken to address one also support the other.3 When a shock pushes them in opposite directions, there is a tradeoff. The recent rise in oil prices tied to the conflict in the Middle East is an example of the latter. An energy price shock can push up inflation while weighing on real incomes and demand. The appropriate response depends on the relative costs of missing each objective and the balance of risks. When the recent energy price shock occurred, inflation in the U.S. was already above our 2 percent target. If inflation expectations risk becoming unanchored, a stronger reaction to inflation is warranted. If price pressures are contained, it may be prudent to prioritize downside risks to employment.

Shocks that affect both aggregate supply and aggregated demand but with differential timing are especially challenging for monetary policymakers. The rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) represents such a shock. Business adoption of AI has risen considerably over the past two years.4 On the demand side, optimism about the technology has been boosting business investment in data centers, advanced computing equipment, and related products. On the supply side, AI is likely to automate some tasks and augment others, which could raise productivity and, in turn, the growth rate of potential output. Currently, the AI buildout is exerting upward pressure on demand and inflation. In the future, advancements in productivity could exert downward pressure on inflation while continuing to support growth. Labor market effects are relevant as well. AI could cause some job displacement, a meaningful challenge for affected households. Like many technological breakthroughs of the past, however, AI could lead to the creation of new jobs and new firms that we have not yet imagined.

Shocks do not arrive in a vacuum. Energy, trade, and AI shocks are all unfolding at once. My colleagues at the Fed and my peers around the world must weigh them simultaneously. Our task is to remain attentive to our respective mandates and to keep the stance of our policies appropriately calibrated to achieve those stated goals. For the U.S. economy, my goal is to restore price stability while maintaining a solid labor market. That outcome will help the economy grow according to its full potential.

Thank you, and I look forward to our discussion.

November 18, 2026

Investing in America’s Underserved Communities

Governor Michael S. Barr

At Making Missing Markets: Catalyzing Capital and Igniting Innovation, Federal Reserve Bank of New York, New York, New York

Share

Thank you to the New York Fed and the organizers of this conference for the opportunity to speak to you today.1 For two reasons I can think of, New York is an excellent place to discuss ways to increase investments in communities where incomplete or missing markets have resulted in less investment than needed. First, it makes sense to initiate a conversation about incentivizing private investment in the center of American finance. Second, New York has been successful over the past few decades in attracting investment in lower-income neighborhoods, reversing forces that once made parts of this city synonymous with urban decline. I first saw that for myself 30 years ago, touring the South Bronx with Treasury Secretary Bob Rubin, where public–private partnerships were building housing, creating businesses and jobs, and making streets safer, block by block. That progress has continued, and the strategies and partnerships we will be discussing today have been a major engine for that success.

The backdrop for community investment, of course, is the economy—how fast it is growing, how well it is creating jobs, and how well incomes are keeping up with the cost of living. So I will start with the economic outlook. Overall, the economy is strong, with low unemployment, solid consumer spending, and robust business investment. But inflation is too high, making it hard for American families to make ends meet. Many families are struggling. And that's why it is so important for the Fed to bring inflation back down to our target of 2 percent in a timely way. If the economy evolves as I currently forecast, I expect it will be appropriate to raise rates further to achieve that goal on behalf of the American people. Of course, I'll continue to monitor the incoming data, the evolving forecast, and the balance of risks in making that assessment.

Strong and sustainable communities are key to American prosperity. When a community is strong and resilient, people see a future there and invest in that future—they buy homes, shop locally, and start new businesses. When hard times hit resilient communities, people can weather the storm and bounce back in part because they believe that they and their communities will bounce back—that jobs and opportunities will return. That's why we at the Fed are committed to building stronger communities. And while economic activity and employment are continuing to grow for the country as a whole, not all families and communities are thriving. Far from it. Wealthier households are doing well, but middle- and lower-income households are struggling, and the economy is leaving some communities behind. Recent estimates are that 44 percent of Americans live in low-income communities, where a fifth or more of people live below the poverty line.2 These communities often lack sufficient investment in housing, business, and infrastructure.

The Fed's recent Survey of Consumer Finances confirms that the majority of households has very little wealth.3 And lower-wealth households are nearly four times as likely to say that they cannot rely on friends and family for financial help or provide that help themselves.4 Think about how that inability to help, or get help, can reverberate across a community. When jobs and opportunities lag in a community, it eats away at the ties that connect families and neighbors. The dignity of work is so key to how many people try to navigate these difficult times, yet in many communities, despite a strong economy, some people are finding it hard to find a good-paying job.

So, what are the kinds of investments that can drive long-term, lasting opportunity? We've seen so many examples of successful public–private partnerships make a difference to households, businesses, and communities. We need the public sector, at all levels of government. We need the private sector. And we need the nonprofit sector. But there are market failures that often prevent the private sector from coming in on its own. For example, information asymmetries can make it hard for commercial banks to see opportunities in low-income areas. When commercial banks can partner with local, state, and national community development groups, including Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFIs), that partnership can help those banks extend credit, investment, and services to those that most need them and, in turn, assist banks in most effectively reaching new customers. These partnerships can help overcome these market failures. Community organizations and national nonprofits can reduce the costs of acquiring and interpreting information about low-income borrowers and communities, they can coordinate lending to overcome collective action problems, and they can also share risks to reduce bank exposures.

By targeting investments to identify root causes of the challenges in a community, public–private partnerships can better help capital find successful strategies at the early stages of the development process. This can help establish the appropriate funding infrastructure to set community-focused projects on a course for long-term investment.5 As President John Williams said in inaugurating this conference in 2024, this event is about investing in solutions that lead to long-term partnerships and sustainable success.6 It means addressing root causes, and also the longer-term outlook for the community. Investments that focus on jobs, housing, schools, infrastructure, and the like are critical.

To illustrate what I mean, I will note a few examples of where communities in the region are building partnerships to attract long-term investments.

Not far from here, in a part of Brooklyn marked by limited housing as well as a lack of employment and healthy food sources, private and public partners came together in a project called City Point, which includes a full-service supermarket, 120 affordable housing units, and retail space for local small businesses. City Point is a great example of how the New Markets Tax Credit (NMTC) can leverage private investment into communities and spur further development.7 I helped launch the bipartisan NMTC a quarter century ago, and I'm so pleased that it was recently made permanent by Congress.

In Middletown, New York, the city is converting a historic railway station, long vacant, into a community center, complete with a Head Start program in a community where childcare options are sparse. The project is being financed in part by the NMTC.

Syracuse, New York, announced earlier this year the creation of a revolving loan fund for mixed-income housing. The fund provided $30 million in seed money, and a group of private partners contributed the remaining $120 million.

The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LITHC) has been a critical tool so important to the recovery of the Bronx and many communities around the country. The LIHTC has helped to create or preserve nearly 4 million units of housing over the past 40 years.8 The Bronx lost roughly a fifth of its housing between 1970 and 1980 and, not coincidentally, about a fifth of its population. But with the help of the Community Reinvestment Act (CRA), the LIHTC, CDFIs, and other factors, it recovered that ground and continues to recover and thrive since then.9

Underpinning all of these projects is the CRA. The CRA has helped to increase lending, investment, and services in low-income communities all across the country. Critical to the CRA's success are banks of all sizes—the biggest banks that serve national markets, regional banks, banks with specialized business models, and community banks—in all kinds of communities, including urban, suburban, small towns, and rural. Banks large and small often partner with CDFIs, national and local community as well as civil rights groups, local chambers of commerce, and the like to make local lending effective. CDFIs are often "boots on the ground" financial providers and can connect stakeholders across sectors. Grants to these and other groups often make the difference between success and failure for banks trying to make a difference in communities—as I said, in rural communities, small towns, big cities, and Native American lands alike.

Today, I've spoken mostly about community development in New York, since we're here in the Second District, but I've seen work like this first-hand all around the country as I've visited the Reserve Bank Districts.

I've listened to bankers, CDFIs, and local entrepreneurs come together in Clarksdale, Mississippi. I've seen affordable housing built by partnerships between tribal governments and local banks on the Flathead and Blackfeet Reservations in Montana. I've seen Working Cities in East Harford, Connecticut, and community-based learning in Portland, Oregon. I've witnessed the transformation, innovation, and entrepreneurial spirit of Detroit, Michigan, and the grit and determination of community development professionals in South Dallas, Texas. Entrepreneurs in Charlotte, North Carolina, told me about the importance of CDFIs, as did leaders in Kansas City, Missouri, and Omaha, Nebraska. I've seen what public–private partnerships mean for employment and small business growth in Cleveland, Ohio, and I've seen a business incubator in Atlanta, Georgia, make a real difference to its local community. In Arizona, I visited with entrepreneurs in Mesa and Tempe who told us about the key role of local community development corporations. And Chicago educational leaders taught me the importance of linking up with local employers to chart job pathways for young people from disadvantaged backgrounds. This good work is happening all over the country, in urban, rural, and tribal communities, and in every Reserve Bank District.

This brings us to why we are gathered today at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. In one part of this building, staff are working every day to help implement monetary policy, help ensure smooth financial markets, and promote a strong economy. But we can't achieve a strong economy without strong communities. Core to the Fed's mission, the Federal Reserve System's Community Development function can help. I applaud the Federal Reserve Bank of New York for bringing attention to the untapped potential of low- and moderate-income communities all across these beautiful, vast, diverse, and entrepreneurial United States. There's lots more we can do together.

Considerations at the Intersection of Payments and Tokenized Markets

Governor Christopher J. Waller

At “Navigating the Future of Banking and Payments,” The Clearing House Annual Conference 2026, New York, New York

Share
Watch Live

Thank you for inviting me to return and speak today.1 I am delighted to be here during a transformational time in payments. Two years ago, I spoke at The Clearing House's annual conference, where I discussed the roles that the private sector and the Federal Reserve play in payments.2 I argued that the public and private sectors have distinct but complementary roles. In particular, I emphasized that the private sector, through competition, is best positioned to develop solutions that benefit businesses and consumers and to fulfill a clear market need.

The Federal Reserve can support these solutions by providing core payment and settlement infrastructure upon which the private sector can innovate, assessing potential risks to financial stability, and regularly engaging with the industry to better understand market challenges. Related to this last role, it's important to note that my remarks today are informed by rich, ongoing discussions I conduct with industry participants across a variety of forums, including a recent payments innovation roundtable I hosted at the Federal Reserve with participants from across traditional and decentralized finance, or DeFi.3

Turning to the timely themes of the conference, many of the sessions focused on the evolving payments landscape, including the state of competition, technological advancements, and readiness for the future. With this backdrop in mind, today I will discuss some considerations for tokenization in the payment system with an eye toward the future and, in particular, the intersection of this technological advancement with existing systems and markets. To be clear, in this context, my focus is on the concept that a financial instrument can be represented digitally in "token" form and transacted on a new platform or technology, such as a distributed ledger technology like blockchain.4

Although the terms "blockchain," "tokenization," and "smart contract" are widely used, to ensure we are on the same page, I want to give a simple explanation of each of these concepts. A blockchain is simply a database that can have a variety of management properties, such as who can hold a copy of the database, who can read the database, who can change the database, and how the database can be updated. The word block means data are added to the existing database in "blocks"—much like adding a new sheet of data to an existing Excel file.5

Tokenization refers to the process of altering the properties of one form of an asset so that it can be transferred and used on a different platform than its current one. For example, if you go to a casino, you cannot use physical currency at the tables or machines. But you can take your cash and buy poker chips to use on the tables. In this world, poker chips are a tokenized version of physical cash that can be used instead of cash.

Finally, a smart contract is simply a piece of code that is embedded in the database and can be triggered to execute an action at a given point of time or under a particular set of conditions. The key aspect of a smart contract is that all conditions of the code must be met simultaneously for the action to occur and be recorded on the blockchain. Smart contracts allow financial trades to be programmable, which is appealing for automating trades.

In any financial trades on a blockchain, there is an object being bought (say, a tokenized share of stock) and there is a tokenized payment instrument being used to settle the trade. For example, stablecoins are starting to be used behind the scenes to settle obligations among participants in card networks. And The Clearing House is one of several groups building a network for clearing and settling tokenized deposits, which are digital transformations of the digits in your checking account that can then be used as a payment instrument to execute a blockchain trade.

Now, let's look a bit closer at how payment innovation has progressed since I was here last. After a sustained period of industry investment and activity, a convergence between blockchain technologies and established markets is clearly under way, and deepening connections between these systems are taking root. Just a few years ago, blockchains were primarily used for crypto-asset trading activities. Today, all kinds of assets—from stocks to bonds to commodities—are being tokenized for use on blockchain networks to take advantage of new capabilities like programmability. And established payment providers, financial institutions, and market infrastructures are leveraging these capabilities. Firms are using these technologies to improve existing services—both customer facing and back office—and introduce new ones.

At any point of convergence, such as those I have described, key questions arise that must be addressed before progress can continue. Let me focus on three areas. First, I will share observations on how tokenization is increasing competition between new and existing players, leading to a variety of solutions and choices for market participants to sort through. Second, I will discuss the importance of building trust in new tokenized services if they are to scale. Third, I will turn to how payments can support safe and efficient tokenized markets, where the same themes of competition, choice, and trust apply. Along the way, I will highlight open questions market participants should consider. I will close with some thoughts on ways the Federal Reserve can continue to support innovation in payments.

Observations and Key Questions
Competition Dynamics
Tokenization is changing the nature of competition in payments. I am a firm believer in the free market and consider it the best testing ground for new, more efficient ways to deliver products and services, including through the introduction of new technologies.6 Blockchains in particular have some distinguishing features that make them ripe ground for competition in payments and financial markets. By operating 24/7/365, open blockchain networks offer a new paradigm for competition with traditional payment rails. In addition, smart contract code is often open source, or available to the general public. You or I could code and launch a smart contract today.

When a technology such as blockchain is introduced, it can often set off a race between new entrants and established firms. Each has relative advantages. The new entrants bring native familiarity with the new technology and its potential to improve efficiency or enable new ways of doing business. But established firms, too, have distinct advantages: They have amassed customer relationships and associated network effects, along with the power of market confidence. History is replete with examples of success from both ends of the field.

This competitive dynamic currently applies to the closely linked domains of payments and financial markets and could lead to more choices for consumers and businesses. Take, for example, DeFi firms, which first emerged to offer complex forms of lending and trading activity using crypto-assets, such as bitcoin and Ethereum. This activity was largely separated from regulated financial markets. As time has passed, there has been a better understanding of the potential for crypto technologies to improve existing payment systems and financial markets.

These new technologies are forcing established firms to compete in a number of ways. In some cases, firms are developing their own blockchains where they maintain final control over the ledger. In other cases, firms are pooling their resources to develop tokenized products and services or blockchains owned by a consortium. In still other cases, experienced institutions are developing new tokenized products and services in partnership with emerging blockchain firms.

Tokenization is still in the early innings of robust competition among new entrants, established firms, and combinations of each. Each model has its own benefits and tradeoffs among control and scale, and time will tell how these play out. Is it necessary for the market to coalesce around one model? I find it more likely that several different solutions will emerge to support different customers and use cases. But a market with many new options raises a further question: Which of them can be trusted?

The Trust Imperative
I now want to turn to what I'll call the trust imperative that is affecting the development of tokenization. For new tokenized services to succeed, they will need more than just technical prowess. They will also need to earn trust through how reliably they operate and how clearly they are governed. It is therefore encouraging to hear that the conversation about which aspects of tokenization need to scale is increasingly focused on trust and governance. Trust is built when transactions occur reliably and securely, both within and across platforms. It is hard to win and easy to lose. In payments, trust is measured not in months and quarters, but in years and even decades. From a public policy perspective, I am acutely aware of the importance of trust in payments.

As traditional financial assets move onto blockchains, tokenized services raise new questions about accountability. Who bears responsibility when fraudsters take advantage of the automatic execution of smart contracts? How will operators of new systems take responsibility for compromised or lost keys, erroneous transfers, and pricing errors? What happens to assets that are stranded on failed blockchains—the inevitable "zombie chains"?7 The recent events around Binance have highlighted some of the issues that can arise from programmable blockchains.8

Answering these questions around trust is not merely a matter of finding the right solutions to purely technical problems. Industry debates are evolving to recognize that clear governance, sound legal frameworks, and common data and standards are just as important, if not more. Assets that move across networks must maintain legal identity, ownership rights, economic terms, compliance obligations, corporate-action treatment, and access to liquidity. Clear legal treatment of tokenized claims, settlement records, custody, insolvency, and resolution will be critical to mainstream institutional adoption—especially when assets are used in the most technically complex, smart contract–enabled arrangements.

Ultimately, the market will determine which solutions deserve broader business and consumer trust. These solutions must strike the right balance between controls and the creative potential of blockchains. As I mentioned earlier, this balance may be use-case specific. For example, systemically important financial transactions may call for the most conservative designs. These designs might trade some of the developer creativity found on purely permissionless, open networks in favor of greater privacy and control.

Integration Considerations
Finally, I want to focus on another area shaping the future of tokenization. How will payment solutions integrate within and across platforms for tokenized assets to support the development of safe and efficient markets? Will tokenized money be accepted and cleared efficiently at par? Here again, competition and trust will be key features of a successful payment system.

As with any market, tokenized finance will need choice in payment methods, with both tokenized and traditional payments having roles to play. Market participants increasingly expect payments to be automated and synchronized with the delivery of assets and services regardless of the time of day or day of the week. For example, imagine an investor buying a tokenized stock on a Saturday, when many traditional systems are closed. Using tokenized money, the payment and the stock could be programmed to change hands simultaneously, reducing settlement delays and the risk that one side delivers and the other does not.

The private sector is quickly building a multi-instrument ecosystem of tokenized money to meet these needs of tokenized markets. In addition to stablecoins, tokenized deposits, tokenized money market funds, and other potential settlement assets are coming to market, each providing distinct benefits and tradeoffs. Stablecoins and tokenized deposits, for example, both enable programmable settlement but differ in issuer model, regulatory treatment, connectivity to traditional systems, and ecosystem reach. Given these choices, some forms of tokenized money may be well suited for certain use cases, like international business payments, while others may be best for high-value transactions in tokenized securities. As a result, corporate treasurers, banks, asset managers, and payment providers may use several tokenized instruments at once to serve different aspects of their business models, in the same way they use multiple rails today.

Choice also matters in settlement methods for tokenized markets. "Atomic" settlement is often cited as a benefit of programmable tokenized money. Broadly speaking, it means a transaction with more than one step that either completes all at once and in full or not at all. That can be useful for complex transactions with multiple parties. But in my conversations with market participants, they cautioned against viewing it as the only settlement model for tokenized markets. Other settlement models, such as net settlement and batch settlement, where only net amounts need to be funded, will remain useful when saving liquidity is a priority, as in high-volume capital market activity.

Given these diverse needs of tokenized markets, I believe traditional rails and the forms of money exchanged on them will continue to play an important role in underpinning trust in the overall payment system. In particular, central bank money need not be used in every transaction or on every platform, but its presence at the foundation of the system is essential.9 Traditional rails—such as those for large-value payments and, more recently, instant payments—support trust because they have proven resilient through market events, and many provide access to the safest forms of dollars through linkages to central bank money. That makes them a reliable backbone for final funds settlement on which innovative services, including tokenized markets, can develop. This is happening today, as traditional rails are used for on- and off-ramping stablecoins and for settlement of tokenized deposit transactions. I encourage further conversation on potential ways that tokenized markets and traditional rails can connect and complement one another.

Reflecting on this landscape, I see a range of payment solutions coexisting to meet the various payment needs of tokenized markets. The market will test different solutions, with the strongest approaches emerging through not only competition but also collaboration. An important question to resolve will be how participants can transition between various forms of money flexibly and safely. I expect continued dialogue between industry participants and the Fed will be helpful for thinking through considerations like cross-system connectivity and trust as the payment system evolves to support tokenized markets.

Looking Ahead
I'll close by sharing some final thoughts on the intersection of tokenization and payments, with an eye toward the future, and then briefly discuss how the Fed can continue to support innovation in the payment system. First, the market is currently in an early period of experimentation and convergence between new and existing systems. The new systems may seem experimental now, but it is possible that market participants will look back on this period in the same way that we look back on the payment, clearing, and settlement technologies of the 19th and 20th centuries. We've come a long way from clearing payments via paper stacks of certificates and telegraph wires.

Second, while the considerations I've discussed today are actively being sorted through, the market is already considering how tokenization and blockchain-based systems will interact with artificial intelligence (AI), particularly AI agents in payments and commerce, a topic I spoke about a few months ago.10 While agentic commerce is still in the early stages, tokenization and AI could be catalysts for one another. Both advancements are also grappling with similar questions on trust, standards, and the use of open or closed networks.

And, lastly, the Federal Reserve will continue to support responsible innovation by regularly engaging with and listening to industry, providing core payment and settlement infrastructure on which all market participants can build, and monitoring risks to financial stability. Where market forces alone cannot address a key challenge, and where it falls within our mandate, the Fed can act as a connector and convener. The goal is to support a market that keeps innovating, remains safe and resilient, and delivers better services for the public.

With all of that in mind, I am keenly focused on how new technologies can affect the structure and functioning of payment systems globally. I look forward to continuing the conversation throughout the conference. Thank you.

Opening Remarks

Chairman Kevin Warsh

At the Productivity and Jobs Task Force Conference, hosted by the Federal Reserve Board and the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, San Francisco, California

Share
Watch Live

Mary, thank you, and welcome.

I'm grateful to Mary and her colleagues for helping to arrange this gathering. This is my very first visit to a Reserve Bank as Chairman. I wouldn't call it a snap inspection, but I'm certainly impressed. Everything I see reflects high expectations and the inquiring, forward-looking spirit that we need in the Federal Reserve System.

It's good to have Fed colleagues with us from around the country. Central banking is a team sport, and I'm pleased you're here. This conference is an expression of the mission we all serve: One Fed. Fit for Purpose. Focused on the Future.

I also want to thank Marc, Chad, and Asha for leading our Productivity and Jobs Task Force. You're giving the scarcest of resources—your time and your commitment. It's a service to the Fed and to our nation, and I'm grateful.

This task force is one of five I have convened as Chairman. These groups are a way of broadening our thinking . . . of inviting robust discussions that take us further and deeper than fleeting data points or daily market moves. Our task forces are an intellectual investment to better equip us for the next policy conjuncture and beyond.

The common attributes of Asha, Marc, and Chad, the reason I asked them to lead, is not just their exceptional insight and experience. It's their battlefield bravery in the world of ideas . . . their intellect and their willingness to think hard about what might happen next. They, in turn, asked me to open the task force discussion to a wide cross section of stakeholders—and that's the purpose of this conference.

As for me, I always feel energized to be in this part of California. I arrived here way back in 1988 as a Stanford undergrad. I remember sitting in Green Library over Thanksgiving break during my freshman year, reading and wrestling with great works from a canon that I had scarcely known existed, beginning my initiation into the world of ideas. It was a special time to be in the Bay Area—neither boom nor bust, a quieter period between tech surges. And, today, just as I was then, I'm keen to listen to and learn from the great people and first-rate minds around me.

The current era may be the most transformational period in my adult lifetime, marked by the arrival of the general-purpose technology called "super intelligence." SI is an entirely new force in economic life and society. It demands our special attention.

Even a few years ago, the idea that we could have, in our pockets, instant access to genius-level intelligence on almost any subject would have seemed far fetched. Today, with the advances of SI, it's happening. The marginal cost of satisfying limitless curiosity—on questions large and small—is falling fast, perhaps even trending toward zero.

In the tradition of endogenous growth theory in economics, ideas are the ultimate resource. If living standards are to advance, conditions must be favorable to the generation of new ideas. It should be no surprise, then, that the United States is leading. Societies that bring forth the best ideas tend to prosper most. The implications for economic policy can hardly be overstated.

While an idea can be costly to discover, it's free to use. And because an idea is nonrival—that is, one person's use doesn't diminish anyone else's—its value grows with the number of people who use it. The Nobel laureate Paul Romer, among others, has shown that a doubling of ideas and other key inputs can more than double output, allowing increasing returns to scale.1

Even so, in the U.S. over the past several decades, breakthrough ideas have been harder to find. By some estimates, research efforts have increased more than 20 times since the 1930s. Yet, as Nick Bloom, Chad Jones, and their coauthors have pointed out, "Research productivity has fallen by an even larger amount."2 The result: Productivity growth has fluctuated, and in recent quarters, it's been reasonably strong. But it hasn't yet broken out definitively and structurally to the upside.

Long-run growth, I should note, is also a function of the rate of increase in the number of people producing new ideas and setting them in motion. The conventional view holds that as population growth slows, there may be fewer new ideas to drive prosperity.3 The increasing challenge of generating breakthrough ideas, combined with slower population growth, helps explain why many in the economics profession, in the post-crisis environment, returned to the theme of secular stagnation.

Does super intelligence dramatically change this picture? Well, there are certainly some indications that it does.

SI might be somewhat different from general-purpose technologies of the past, such as the internal combustion engine or electricity. Those inventions made for decades of follow-on innovation but did not themselves automate the creation of ideas. What might set SI apart, as an economic influence, is that it could supercharge idea generation. It appears to be changing the method—and the speed—of innovation. If idea generation broadens, growth could be sustained at a level substantially higher than the long-run average.4

For all of that, however, humans should not be underestimated. There is no replacing the minds and the hands of men and women. Machines that can amass or even exceed human knowledge are still machines. They do not take the place of human judgment, morality, or responsibility. Humans are the designers and the builders, and humans must be the deciders.

For our country in this era, consider how much is on the line. I remember Bob Mundell, another Nobel recipient, saying years ago that the most important fact of the twentieth century was the rise of the United States as a superpower.5 For us today, that is more than a fact of history—it is a charge to keep. And so we must ask, can you be a superpower without super intelligence?

My Fed colleagues and I are not at this conference as futurists to speculate on each new turn technology might take. And we're certainly not here to make predictions. We can't know the future and ought not to pretend otherwise.

Our job is to prepare for whatever comes next. That means contingency planning, which becomes only more important when the world is changing this fast. Our aim today is a humble one: to ask the right questions.

As policymakers, we have to be mindful that great opportunities rarely come risk free. And the risks in this case, if overlooked, can be highly disruptive and economically costly. SI empowers all, including bad actors, so we are thinking especially of critical infrastructures and their potential vulnerabilities.

And, of course, I think of economic policy in the era of SI. At the Fed, our role in all this is neither advocacy nor opposition. Our responsibility is to recognize that the widespread adoption of this technology will have significant impact on the economy, with important implications for monetary policy down the road.

The big questions are about magnitude, scope, and timing. Will this new technology deliver a rise in total factor productivity? Will the democratization of specialized knowledge significantly raise standards of living? When will the surge start, and when will it taper? Will it happen sooner and faster here than in other countries? Will adversaries race to deploy SI, intending to do harm to our economy?

The answers will matter greatly—and that is why we're thinking about them now, in a sober and serious way. I expect much more clarity with the passing of time.

Still, it's worth considering the range of economic outcomes we could see over the next several years, depending on how SI progresses. The Board's staff has begun to evaluate various contingencies, applying stylized, model-based frameworks to generate scenarios.

These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts. Moreover, many other outcomes—probably including one that will prove most accurate—could be drawn. Consider them a starting point for the very useful discussions we've got lined up for today.

At one end, SI leads to an increase in productivity and economic gains that are shared broadly across labor and the owners of capital. In that event, real GDP growth rises persistently above its long-run average, and the U.S. stays close to full employment.

Other outcomes, however, could be less positive. An SI-driven expansion could bring substantial labor market disruption as some jobs are displaced, rather than complemented, by technology. GDP growth would still rise above its long-term average. But widespread automation, combined with labor market frictions, could push the jobless rate above levels consistent with full employment.

Or SI could disappoint. After significant capital investment and stock market gains, expected returns could turn down and equity valuations could fall, leading to an economic slowdown and a more notable rise in unemployment. In time, the economy would recover, but without the productivity gains.

I stress that while I'm not here to offer predictions, I'm not exactly impartial about American leadership in a time so filled with both challenge and possibility.

I thought back recently to my days at the Hoover Institution and to a collaboration with my fine mentor and colleague John Cogan. As we wrote some years ago: "If we meet the challenges before us, America has a bright future with strong, durable economic growth, abundant economic opportunities, vibrant and secure communities, and a healthy environment. Future generations will inherit a dynamic economy capable of delivering technological advancements that will extend lives and promote human flourishing."6

That is the goal, the ultimate scenario, that all of us here hope to see. It's a privilege to be a policymaker at a time of such consequence—and it's an encouraging sign to be in the company of so many serious and capable men and women.

Thank you all very much.

Some Potential Financial Stability Implications of Artificial Intelligence

Governor Michael S. Barr

At the Seventh Annual Association for Computing Machinery International Conference on Artificial Intelligence in Finance, Milan, Italy

Share
Watch Live

Thank you for the opportunity to speak to you today.1 This is the fourth speech I have given in the past year devoted exclusively to artificial intelligence (AI), and most of the others have also addressed it in some way. The effects of AI on people and economies around the globe are among the most important issues we are facing today.

Let me stipulate at the outset that I am an optimist about the long-term promise of AI to empower individuals, businesses, and institutions across society. I remain hopeful that if we manage today's challenges and tomorrow's potential disruptions forcefully and effectively, that AI will lift up people, make them more productive, and broadly raise living standards—that most people will learn more and earn more. One of the reasons I'm optimistic about this technology is that I'm also optimistic about the capacity of society to understand and respond to the changes that AI will bring—to engage in a constructive dialogue about ensuring that the benefits from AI are not overwhelmed by the disruptions it will inevitably bring and the risks it may pose.

This conference is part of that dialogue, directed at questions that I believe will be crucial in ensuring that AI is a positive force in the financial system that empowers individuals to better manage their finances and access credit and services, lowers costs and enhances the capabilities of financial service providers, and also makes markets function more effectively. But AI can only succeed in strengthening our financial system in this way if it also supports financial stability, something that is indispensable to a healthy economy.

As has been the case for some time, there are important insights about AI and financial stability in the Federal Reserve's latest Financial Stability Report (FSR) issued on Friday. This edition of the twice-yearly report focuses on the AI investment boom and research by the Fed's staff to assess the degree of leverage in some of the innovative debt structures being employed to finance this growth. I will recap the report's findings and then turn to a discussion of what economic research can tell us about the benefits and risks of integrating AI into financial markets, focusing on wholesale trading. Next, I will examine the potential benefits and risks of AI's effect on cybersecurity and then finally consider what AI might mean for consumer and investor protection.

The AI Investment Boom
Anyone watching the news or their retirement plans these past couple of weeks is aware of the questions that investors have raised about the sustainability of the AI investment boom. I should issue the usual disclaimer here that policymakers on the Federal Reserve Board do not target levels of financial markets. Markets go up, and markets go down, and I assiduously avoid commenting on the ups and downs in markets. We do assess longer-term issues that relate to financial stability, and the latest FSR covers several.

In a previous speech, I discussed three different scenarios summarizing the wide range of possible outcomes for how much AI could alter the economy.2 In one possible scenario among several, even if AI turns out to be a transformative innovation that vastly improves productivity, a key question is whether investors will see returns on the AI buildout on a timeline and to an extent consistent with their expectations or whether a different outcome could lead to a significant repricing. The potential investment losses that could result from a large-scale repricing could lower output growth in two ways: first, from the direct effect of a drop in investment, which is a component of gross domestic product; and, second, from the knock-on effects of this loss of wealth in lowering consumer and potentially business spending. As discussed in the latest FSR, the equity premium, a rough measure of the additional return that investors require for holding stocks relative to risk-free bonds, is at low levels relative to historical norms, though not nearly as low as the levels reached during the dot-com stock market boom.3 This is just one indicator to assess where broad measures of equity valuations are relative to historical norms.

Leverage in the Data Center Buildout
In addition to the question of equity valuations, the AI buildout has been increasingly funded by debt. The Federal Reserve's FSR delves into three ways in which the financing of AI data centers and related investments, which it calls "advanced computing debt," could affect financial stability: (1) the rapid growth of this debt, (2) circular financing networks, and (3) other complex financial obligations. In the case of a significant repricing of AI investments, stress from these three aspects of AI financing could amplify the repricing of AI investments.

On-balance-sheet debt of the seven largest AI firms has been growing significantly in 2026 and is expected to exceed $800 billion before year's end. The growth of debt at these firms brings to mind the growth of debt in the telecom investment cycle of the late 1990s and early 2000s. The FSR compares hyperscaler firms' cumulative capital spending and debt issuance since 2020 with those of all firms in the telecommunications industry during the broadband investment cycle that started in 1995. While the hyperscalers have yet to surpass their comparators of the earlier boom, the recent acceleration in their capital expenditures suggests they may do so soon.4

While these firms' on-balance-sheet debt levels have grown substantially, their other obligations, such as long-term contracts, lease commitments, and residual value guarantees, have grown even more rapidly. The FSR estimates that the potential maximum nominal value of these types of obligations, or off-balance-sheet liabilities, now exceeds $3 trillion.5 A significant share of these obligations results from complex arrangements in which hyperscalers and other large firms with strong credit ratings indirectly support the financing of projects that involve other firms with weaker credit, or provide support for public utility investments. These off-balance-sheet liabilities include obligations relating to energy supply contracts, long-term data center and compute leases, and a wide variety of off-balance-sheet financing structures. As I have discussed in previous speeches, this financial complexity can be opaque to unsophisticated investors, and that opacity can exacerbate selloffs when investor sentiment shifts, as it is hard to know where risk resides. As a result, the development of these substantial off-balance-sheet structures warrants close monitoring. Perhaps just as important, in addition to the hyperscalers, lower-credit-quality firms most dependent on external funding have seen their cost of capital rise significantly in recent months and, as a result, are turning to more complex asset-backed financings.

In addition, the hyperscaler firms and large graphics processing unit (GPU) producers also have over $100 billion in cumulative equity investments in large language model (LLM) labs and cloud-computing start-ups. These equity investments represent a form of "circular financing" in which hyperscaler firms and GPU makers invest capital in smaller firms, and the smaller firms turn around and use these funds to rent or purchase GPUs and compute from the firm that made the investment. These investments represent a relatively small share of the hyperscalers' and GPU producers' balance sheets, but they are critical for the smaller firms receiving the investments. For the AI buildout investments to ultimately become economically viable, the smaller firms will eventually have to replace that funding from their suppliers, the hyperscalers and GPU makers, with revenue from paying customers.

Another indirect form of circular financing in which the hyperscalers and GPU producers support purchases of their products and services is the use of collateral guarantees for asset-based financings. As the firms most dependent on external financing bump up against financing constraints, they shift from issuing corporate bonds to issuing asset-based financing. To support the credit of these financings, the larger higher-credit firms often guarantee the resale value for the data centers or GPUs that are used as collateral to back the financings. These guarantees, or collateral backstops, are reported as off-balance-sheet liabilities because they represent the degree to which hyperscalers and GPU producers might absorb losses in the event of defaults and collateral liquidation.

As we take a step back and consider the enormous scope of AI-related investment in capital stock, it is helpful to consider how it compares to that for previous technology cycles. By one estimate, the capital investment associated with the AI buildout could plausibly rival or even exceed the investments associated with previous infrastructure investment booms, including those associated with railroads in the 1870s, highways in the 1950s and '60s, and fiber-optic cable in the 1990s.6 Each of these investment booms may have felt overbuilt at some point, but today we are glad to have the railroad, highway, and fiber-optic capacity that they put in place. Some of those earlier booms fizzled, with consequences for the broader financial system. Given the still-limited amount of debt backing the current boom, I'm less concerned about direct losses. However, with leverage at hedge funds and broader risk appetite apparently quite high, a sharp reversal could lead to rapid deleveraging and, potentially, self-reinforcing fire sales. Further, the leverage used in equity strategies has also increased in recent years. This leverage is provided, in part, via the prime brokerage services of large dealers. Thus, while the high levels of capital at the largest banks provide a bulwark against spillovers, counterparty exposures could rise in a period of equity market volatility, so it's important to keep monitoring exposures, including exposures to counterparties that have taken large positions in AI.

Moreover, the bulk of a data center's cost comes from the GPUs and other specialized chips used in AI. One concern is that these specialized chips may depreciate quickly as newer technologies overtake them, such that the capacity they represent will not be of much use in the future. Currently, the lease rates of older vintages of chips appear to be holding up, suggesting that there are uses even for computing technology behind the cutting edge, but those trends are worth monitoring. It is hard to say what the other parts of the AI-related capital stock—substations, high-voltage connections, and grid interconnection—may be used for if demand for their services disappoints. In some areas of the country, no doubt, extra power and network capacity will be useful regardless of the future of AI. In other areas, it may take years for excess capacity to become useful, as was the case with "dark fiber" in some areas after the 1990s boom and bust. Or maybe there will be no AI investment fizzle but a continued boom, and our demand for AI services will make full use of the capital being installed now and then some. Only time will tell.

Unintended Consequences of AI in Wholesale Securities Trading
Another possible effect of AI on the financial system is the technology's potential to reshape trading in financial markets, a topic addressed by previous FSRs.7 Generative AI is a powerful tool that can process broader and richer data and information and apply more complex logic than traditional trading algorithms. It can lower barriers to accessing advanced trading technology and increase diversity of market participants. And it can also help improve market surveillance. Each of these capacities can improve market efficiency, integrity, and functioning.

As with any new technology, however, there are risks. While it is my understanding that generative AI is not yet being used for autonomous trading at large scale, we need to be prepared to understand the risks and implications of its adoption.8 Many of the most important questions about how AI will affect financial markets will be answered not in the abstract, but in the technical choices made about model design, training data, testing, and deployment.

While AI can democratize access to trading expertise and technology, it could also increase information asymmetry and market concentration.9 The state-of-the art systems are expensive and require massive investment.10 This could confer an advantage on the largest firms and concentrate market power among them.11 Even when AI tools are widely available, effectively using them requires considerable skill, potentially favoring larger incumbents.12

The implications for price efficiency of the integration of AI into financial market trading are ambiguous.13 In his 2026 Jackson Hole paper, Markus Brunnermeier cautioned that securities prices may also become less informative because of asymmetric understanding: AI agents understand humans and can anticipate their actions, but humans do not understand AI agents as well and cannot anticipate what they might do.14

Market Herding and a Possible Stampede
If traders rely on a small set of similar AI models trained on similar data, they face the risk of herding behavior—trading strategies could become similar and synchronize reactions to news and other information.15 Algorithm flaws can cause failures, but a market with large numbers of similar models that may have the same flaw risks failures with systemic consequences.16 Moreover, bad actors could intentionally manipulate trades, quotes, or news and potentially mislead similar AI trading systems at the same time, amplifying market disruptions.17

AI models may echo unfortunate human tendencies. For example, some theoretical research shows that some reinforcement-learning algorithms, so called Q-learning trading algorithms, can autonomously develop collusive behavior without explicit intent.18 Such self-learning algorithms can also autonomously learn to manipulate markets—for example, through spoofing, which is placing orders designed to create a false impression of market demand—or adopt trading strategies that create and exploit bubbles, thereby destabilizing markets.19 To attempt to combat such potential risks, exchanges are deploying sophisticated machine learning and AI tools themselves for market surveillance.20 Some trading venues are already acting to reduce risks from the "black box" nature of AI-enabled trading algorithms.21

Since we all have an interest in safe, fair, and efficient markets, we need to think through the risks and implications before any new technology is integrated into trading activity or market infrastructure more broadly. When that technology is still not fully understood, and, in fact, its development may be galloping ahead of our understanding, some caution is warranted.

AI and Cyber Risk
Incidents this year have shown the risks from AI models acting autonomously, not in alignment with the goals of their human creators, including cyber risks. Given a goal, AI models have treated security boundaries as obstacles to overcome rather than rules to be followed, raising questions about how effective rules will be even against AI agents instructed to follow them. AI models have exploited some of the same vulnerabilities found by human cyberattackers, such as the heavy reliance of many competing firms on a shared platform provider. AI-enabled cyberattackers have demonstrated that they are able to exploit vulnerabilities that were previously thought too difficult to overcome, and thus not worth fixing, even if they were known in advance. And AI models are also revealing vulnerabilities no one dreamed of just a few years ago. I have spoken before, for example, about how AI can raise cyber risks through its use in creating deepfakes.22

Just like the attackers, cyber defenders are employing AI, finding and patching vulnerabilities much faster than humans ever could. But recent incidents raise the question of whether defenders could always be at a disadvantage. Attackers only need to be right once, while defenders need to be right all the time. Cyber defense must be consistently successful, while cyber offense can experiment and fail repeatedly until something works.

All of this is to say that the work of defenders is hard and getting harder given the speed of model innovation. The ability of both the private sector and government to manage cyber risk and effectively manage events as they occur will remain vital to maintaining financial stability. We all have a stake in winning this race against cyberattackers, because none of us can succeed without a strong and safe financial system. I hope those competing for a piece of the future of AI in finance can collaborate to help cyber defense stay ahead of cyberattacks.

AI in Consumer Finance
AI is likely to play a growing role in consumer finance, as it will in most retail businesses, especially those where interactions with customers don't need to involve a human. AI got a head start in finance over many other sectors in providing customer assistance, and consumers are themselves driving adoption, employing chatbots, for example, as a source of investment advice. In many cases, this advice is being dispensed without the structured protections, regulation, and oversight applied when the source of that advice is a financial institution. In addition to individuals turning to chatbots on their own, financial-sector firms are using AI to improve their financial advice.

The increasing use of AI by customers and financial firms raises important investor-protection concerns, such as conflicts of interest, the quality of investment recommendations, and firms' obligations to act in investors' best interests. AI systems may generate recommendations based not only on an investor's financial circumstances and investment objectives, but also on factors that advance a firm's commercial interests, such as promoting proprietary products, generating transaction fees, or encouraging more frequent trading. These conflicts may be embedded in algorithms and difficult for brokers and advisers, investors, or regulators to identify or understand. In addition, AI systems may rely on incomplete or biased data, generate inaccurate information, or recommend investments that are inconsistent with an investor's risk tolerance, financial circumstances, or investment horizon.

Institutions may increasingly use AI to expand household credit access through alternative data analysis. However, AI poses significant compliance challenges. The technology can function as a black box, making it difficult to explain adverse decisions or ensure compliance with fair lending laws.23 Alternative data sources such as rental payments, utility bills, or employment patterns may expand access to credit if they are used appropriately, but they may also inadvertently serve as proxies for protected characteristics like race or national origin. Thus, care must be taken. Unlike traditional credit reports, consumers often cannot review or dispute this information.

To use AI responsibly, institutions must invest in explainable AI techniques, implement continuous fairness monitoring, maintain detailed model validation records, and provide accessible dispute mechanisms. AI providers must identify biases in training data and monitor outputs to prevent amplifying inequalities or mispricing risks. Those of us who enforce consumer protection laws must remain equally vigilant.

New risks continue to surface. For example, major banks now report finding AI agents attempting to take actions in retail customer accounts, without the bank being able to verify authenticity, consent, and alignment with customer instructions. These incidents underscore that AI in financial services can only succeed long term with appropriate guardrails: strict authentication, explicit consent verification for specific actions, technical controls limiting agent capabilities, and both rules- and values-based constraints. We need technological innovations that ensure AI agents follow established rules and values, especially critical as agentic commerce expands. In addition, AI agents may accelerate deposit outflows during stress periods. Where customers once needed to execute transfers, AI agents could instantaneously detect yield differentials or stability signals and autonomously move funds at high speed, potentially accelerating runs that unfold in minutes, overwhelming traditional liquidity management and regulatory intervention timelines.

Conclusion
I have discussed a number of issues related to integrating AI into the financial system. Among the key challenges are the practical constraints of using AI for core business activities in the financial sector, as firms need to ensure that the resulting processes and outcomes are consistent with relevant laws and appropriate risk management. Large institutions are exploring the use of generative AI, including agentic AI, in their business models—but doing so requires care. To successfully leverage the potential of generative AI on a sustainable basis, decisions based on those models must be well controlled, numerically and legally precise, explainable, and replicable. AI developers still struggle to some extent with all of those criteria. We need to reduce the risk that AI reinforces biases in consumer lending. And we also need to guard against the risks that could result from the use of AI in financial markets including collusion, market manipulation, or trading strategies that result in significant market volatility or even systemic risk. Opportunities abound for using AI to improve access and efficiency in our financial system in ways that benefit providers and customers. I believe we can and must ensure that this proceeds in ways that protect users and support financial stability.

New Technology, Old Questions: Productivity Puzzles and the Macroeconomy

Vice Chair Philip N. Jefferson

At the University of California, San Diego Economic Roundtable, San Diego, California

Share

Thank you, Jim, for that kind introduction, and thank you to UC San Diego for hosting me.1 It is a pleasure to be speaking to this group that includes students, faculty, and business leaders. The topics I want to focus on today are relevant for all of us: How could rapid technological change affect our economy, and what could that mean for monetary policy?

I will start by discussing an earlier period of major technological advances and what lessons we might distill from it for the current juncture. Next, I will turn to what evidence we have regarding the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI).2 I will consider how widely these tools are being used, in which sectors, and what recent research tells us about their potential effects on productivity. Then, I will consider the implications of rapid technological change for the economy more broadly, including for aggregate supply and demand, for inflation, and for the labor market. Finally, I will discuss briefly my thinking about the economic outlook and monetary policy.

Productivity Puzzles: Then and Now
Nobel Prize-winning economist Robert Solow famously wrote in 1987 that "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics."3 He may have been expecting too much from the computers he saw. While 32 percent of workers used computers at work in 1989, the computers were not very powerful. To illustrate, the processor in your phone today can do about as many calculations per second as 10 million 1987 vintage personal computers (PCs).4 In the aggregate, national spending on computers—shown in figure 1—was barely detectable at the time, despite PCs being an important part of the zeitgeist.5 Ten years later, computer investment had taken off and some observers, notably Chairman Greenspan, believed they could see it in the productivity statistics, though the link between the two was only established later.6 Investment in information technology (IT) capital—computers, software, and communications equipment—increased the annual growth rate of labor productivity about 3/4 percentage point in the late 1990s.7

While that expansion of the IT capital stock directly boosted worker productivity, there was more than that to the IT boom. Businesses also reorganized their operations around computers, which further supported productivity growth. Advancements in technology changed the cost of creating, storing, and communicating information and provided an opportunity for firms to rethink their scope, scale, and production functions.8 E-commerce, a wave of globalization, and software in the cloud eventually emerged from that process. One sign that reorganization was beneficial and significant was the rapid rise in the amount of output per unit of input outside the IT sector in the early 2000s. That productivity growth was not purely due to investing in more capital; instead, it also reflected increases in what economists call total factor productivity, or TFP.9

The seeds of the 1990s productivity boom were planted long before Solow's observations in 1987. Basic research that laid the foundations for modern computing included the invention of the transistor in 1947 and the Fortran compiler in 1954. For decades, the applied research by firms that followed yielded practical applications for these advances, relentlessly improving information technology.

This pattern suggests a simple model of productivity growth. Basic research, conducted without a definite application in mind, expands our knowledge about the world. Applied research develops practical industrial applications, which increase productivity and drive down the cost of production. When costs decrease in those industries, prices tend to fall as well, especially in the presence of competition. Over time, firms intensify their use of lower-priced capital goods produced by higher productivity, a process known as "capital deepening." Finally, firms using that capital rethink their approach to production, further increasing TFP and, perhaps, starting the investment cycle again. As the computing example illustrates, this process takes time for its full effect to be felt and measured, particularly for technologies that require business reorganization

It may be reasonable to apply this model to the development of AI. The generative models in focus today can be traced back to basic research that began soon after the transistor was invented.10 Practical applications emerged many years later. For example, the U.S. Postal Service began using AI to read hand-addressed envelopes in the 1990s.11 Usage took off, especially in the information and finance sectors, about 10 years later with the emergence of machine-learning models. Finally, after incredible improvements and training on massive data sets—all the text on the worldwide web, by some accounts—large language models are now good enough to support today's popular chatbots, coding assistants, and smart devices as well as a host of less visible applications like scientific research.

Today's world mirrors the one Bob Solow observed. The new technology seems to be everywhere: Generative AI dominates the news headlines, sits on our phones, and pops up in our software and customer service calls. It may have far-reaching implications for workers, firms, and government agencies, including central banks. But the old questions continue to haunt: How much are businesses and workers actually using it, and is that use affecting the productivity statistics? Now, I'll review some preliminary evidence on these questions.

Paths of Adoption
Recent surveys find that an appreciable and rising share of U.S. firms and workers are using generative AI. Surveys of workers have found that 40 to 50 percent of working adults are using the technology in their jobs, as shown by the blue line in figure 2.12 Among those workers who do use AI at work, however, only a modest fraction uses it every day, as the red dashed line shows.

In addition, the adoption rate appears to vary widely across industries. While indicators of business adoption vary widely depending on methodology, the Census Bureau's survey of a nationally representative sample of U.S. firms illustrates the cross-industry differences.13 As shown in figure 3, their survey indicates that, as of October 2026, roughly 25 percent of all U.S. firms, shown by the red bar, were using the technology in at least one business function as of the middle of this year. That share was closer to 40 percent in knowledge-intensive sectors such as professional services and information sectors. At the other end of the spectrum, it was around 10 percent for the mining and leisure and hospitality sectors.

Thus, compared with more mature technologies like the personal computer, AI is not truly everywhere—in the sense of being fully integrated into most firms' and workers' workflows across the economy. Rather, it appears that we are at an earlier stage that is a natural part of the long cycle of innovation and deployment. The new technology has shown its capability, and firms and workers are actively using it and exploring its possibilities, with some industries, firms, and occupations much farther ahead in this process than others.

Is the adoption we have seen thus far showing up in aggregate productivity statistics? Productivity growth has in fact moved up in recent years, but it's too early to say what role AI played in that development. Productivity measures are so volatile that economists often look through quarterly or even annual swings by looking at long trailing averages. Labor productivity growth averaged 2.2 percent between 2020 and 2025, a welcome step up from the modest 1.5 percent average growth rate seen over the previous business cycle. However, this surge in productivity began before the debut of popular AI chatbots in 2022 and likely reflects a variety of factors, including a pickup in new business formation. Teasing out the contribution of AI to recent productivity growth is not straightforward. We'll need to see more data. Retrospectively, research tied the 1990s productivity boom to IT, but we didn't know that for sure in real time.

What about the microeconomic evidence? There is a growing body of evidence that the new technology is lifting efficiency directly and substantially for some specific tasks, particularly where it can most readily substitute for humans. For example, one influential academic study found that generative AI tools significantly improved the ability of some customer support employees to resolve issues.14

At the same time, the evidence about how task-level efficiency gains are affecting broader productivity appears mixed. On the one hand, some recent studies have found positive associations between productivity growth and adoption of or exposure to AI at the firm level.15 On the other hand, some surveys that attempt to measure causality more directly have found only modest effects so far. For example, one recent study surveyed senior managers and estimated that AI has boosted sales per employee by just 1/2 percent over the past three years.16

What could explain this apparent disconnect between task-level and firm-level gains? One likely reason is that, even among firms that use AI, the scope remains limited, as they are still experimenting and learning. One recent study found that among adopting firms, most are using AI in at most a few business functions, most commonly sales and marketing, strategy and business development, and information technology.17 Another possible explanation is that even for the most natural use cases, efficiency gains in one domain go hand-in-hand with offsetting costs, new demands, or bottlenecks elsewhere. For example, some firms report that AI-generated code contains more errors, so more debugging work is necessary.

That said, even if only a small minority of applications have delivered substantial productivity gains thus far, broader improvements may well materialize over time. Firms and workers will likely broaden and intensify their use of AI, as well as reorganize production and workflows. And successful firms could gain a competitive advantage, enabling them to expand and draw labor and capital away from competing firms.

All told, my judgement is that the evidence so far appears consistent with a contribution to aggregate productivity that is limited, but with the potential to grow quite substantially, broadly in line with historical episodes of technological breakthroughs. That said, the magnitude and timing of forthcoming productivity gains remain highly uncertain.

Implications for the Macroeconomy
What does all this mean for my thinking about macroeconomic activity? My baseline view is that adoption of new technology will ultimately lift the economy's productive capacity substantially but gradually. Expectations of those longer-term gains on the supply side of the economy are already increasing aggregate demand significantly. A substantial portion of the rise in equity prices over the past few years can be attributed to enthusiasm around the new technology. That added wealth appears to be raising household spending. Firms are building and equipping new data centers on a very large scale to support model training and inference, driving rapid growth in aggregate business investment.18 This spending is putting upward pressure on resource utilization and the general price level. In addition, because investment demand is concentrated in some sectors where supply has struggled to keep pace, such as memory and logic chips, it has also contributed to steep increases in prices of specific goods. Over the medium term, the supply of these goods should catch up, and the broader boost to aggregate productivity from adoption should materialize.19 Both of those developments should ease the upward pressure on prices we see today.

While the new technology should ultimately generate higher output and income in the aggregate, I recognize that this transition could be painful for some workers and businesses. Firms that lag in adoption could be at a competitive disadvantage, and their long-term viability could be at risk. And even at firms that successfully adopt the technology, workers who specialize in tasks that AI can perform more efficiently—such as certain aspects of software development—may face job displacement.

One risk to my baseline outlook is that the new technology could displace workers much faster than they can adapt and retrain, which could result in an increase in unemployment. In this scenario, a key question for monetary policy would be how much of this increase is structural—reflecting a persistent mismatch between worker skills and firms' needs—and therefore likely to persist even when the economy is operating close to its full potential. Monetary policy can affect cyclical conditions, like a temporary downturn in employment and spending, but it cannot address the structural factors that determine long-run rates of employment.

As a monetary policymaker, I must also consider the potential effects of the new technology on the longer-run neutral rate of interest, often called r*. If the neutral rate rises, then for any given level of the federal funds rate, policy effectively becomes more accommodative. Conversely, if the neutral rate declines, then policy effectively becomes more restrictive. If the new technology leads to permanently higher levels of productivity growth, it may increase firms' desire to invest and, hence, their demand for funding, raising the level of interest rates. As I discussed earlier, higher productivity growth may also discourage household savings and raise consumption by increasing expected future income. Under these circumstances, to reconcile the increase in investment with reduced savings, the neutral rate would likely rise. However, predicting changes in the neutral rate of interest is challenging, given the historically noisy relationship between productivity growth and real interest rates. Policymakers must remain attentive to real-time developments and potential future developments to ensure that the stance of monetary policy remains appropriately calibrated.

Economic Outlook
While the rapid pace of technological change has understandably captured the public's attention, as a monetary policymaker my charge is to consider the broader economy, and not just a single sector. Therefore, I will use the last portion of my talk to discuss my current economic outlook for the U.S. and the stance of monetary policy.

The economy has gained momentum this year, expanding at a pace roughly in line with its potential. That consistent growth is notable because the economy has faced a cascade of shocks, including an energy shock, and significant changes in trade policy, in addition to the technology buildout. As you can see in figure 4, GDP expanded at a 3.4 percent pace in the third quarter. Business investment has been quite strong. Consumer spending has remained resilient. I expect the expansion will continue roughly in line with potential output next year.

A broad range of recent data from the labor market shows conditions have stabilized. Figure 5 shows that the unemployment rate has largely moved sideways this year, settling at 4.1 percent in October. Layoffs remain low, and job openings have moved a bit higher. While job creation has been volatile, payroll gains have broadened to many sectors in recent months, which I find encouraging. I expect the unemployment rate to stay around its current level in the near term—a level I view as consistent with maximum employment. The risks to my economic output and employment forecasts are roughly balanced. An increase in uncertainty tied to Middle East tensions may weigh on activity and hiring, while a further surge in investment spending could support stronger growth.

Turning to the price-stability side of our dual mandate, inflation remains notably above the Federal Open Market Committee's (FOMC) 2 percent target. The 12-month change in the personal consumption expenditures price index, the blue line in figure 6, was 3.6 percent in September. Higher energy costs are one of the drivers of elevated inflation. Oil prices have been quite volatile, and global energy supplies have come under renewed strain, reflecting heightened geopolitical tensions. I remain concerned about the risk of higher energy prices leading to a persistent rise in inflation more broadly. The 12-month change in core PCE inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy prices and is shown by the dashed red dashed line, was 3.1 percent in September. Indirectly, higher energy costs are showing through in certain areas including airfares and transportation, and the boom in demand for new technologies has been driving unusually strong cost increases for consumer goods and services that depend on the same inputs, like memory. Data received so far for October remains consistent with well above-target inflation.

I am also paying close attention to inflation expectations. Survey-based, short-term measures of inflation expectations are elevated, mainly reflecting higher energy prices. Most measures of longer-term inflation expectations, however, have remained stable at levels consistent with 2 percent inflation.

At the FOMC policy meeting last month, I joined the majority of my colleagues in voting to hold the federal funds rate steady, as shown in figure 7. Data received since that meeting have been consistent with economic activity and labor market conditions remaining broadly solid, while inflation readings show persistent price pressures. Accordingly, I see the trajectory of inflation as providing the more important signal for determining the appropriate stance of monetary policy.  Going forward, I believe that further policy adjustments should be determined by carefully examining trends in the data, the evolving outlook, and the balance of risks.

Conclusion
In conclusion, I will leave you with this thought: New technologies cause us to revisit old but very important questions. The paths from innovation to adoption and adaptation to durable productivity gains can be uneven and unpredictable. Some would even say, puzzling.

New technologies command our attention long before they affect measured productivity. They cause us to rethink our understanding of how the economy works. Re-understanding our economy makes the process of monetary policymaking exciting and challenging. Thankfully, through it all, our mission at the Fed is clear. Deliver on the dual mandate given to us by Congress: maximum employment and price stability. These are the goals of every action we take.

Thank you for your time today.

American Inequality and the Future of AI

Governor Michael S. Barr

At Yale University, New Haven, Connecticut

Share
Watch Live

Thank you for the opportunity to speak to you today.1 In a speech earlier this year, I explored scenarios in which artificial intelligence (AI) could affect economic inequality, for better or for worse.2 My focus in those remarks was on the AI side of this relationship, and today I would like to return to this topic by first providing some important context about inequality in the United States.

Inequality is an aspect of every economy, and understanding the extent and evolution of inequality in the United States can help in forming a clear view of the economic outlook and how the economy is likely to react to new developments. Widening inequality may also, depending on many factors, reflect a decline in economic opportunity for many people, which can limit the economy's productive capacity.

Today, I will begin by summarizing the current state of economic inequality in the United States, assisted by new results from a long-running survey of households conducted by the Federal Reserve, and then show where this fits into the history of inequality over the past century or so. I will explore some of the forces that have shaped inequality, and what these forces may indicate about how AI is likely to affect the way wealth and income are distributed.

Wealth and Income Inequality Today
Up-to-date and detailed information about inequality in the United States comes from the latest edition of the Federal Reserve's triennial Survey of Consumer Finances, conducted in 2025 and published just last month.3 The survey confirms that both wealth and income are highly concentrated and that inequality is greater for wealth. The top 10 percent by income of households receive almost one-half of all income in the United States and the top 1 percent receive about one-fifth of income.4 As for wealth, the wealthiest 10 percent of households hold nearly three-fourths of all wealth, and the top 1 percent hold about one-third. By contrast, the entire lower half of households, as measured by wealth holdings, have only 2 percent of wealth. Low wealth means it's also harder to get by every day. Another Fed survey of household finances conducted each year, the Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking, finds that 31 percent of respondents in 2025 didn't have the cash to cover an emergency expense of $500 or more.5

Wealth is even more highly concentrated among the very wealthiest. One-third of wealth is held by the upper 1 percent—that's nearly $60 trillion. There is even more concentration at the very top. The households in the upper 0.1 percent, which is only 135,000 individuals and families, own one-sixth of our nation's wealth. A household at the lower edge of the top 1 percent has 75 times as much wealth as the median household in the middle of the distribution—$16.3 million compared with $215,000 for a household in the middle. A household at the lower edge of the 0.1 percent of the wealthiest has 300 times as much wealth as the median household.

Wealth concentration has increased at the very top. Using Forbes magazine's tally of the 400 wealthiest individuals, together with data from the Survey of Consumer Finances, a rough estimate is that their share of total wealth rose from 3 percent in 2022 to 4 percent in the most recent survey. Forbes counts nearly 1,000 U.S. billionaires.6

Digging into the sources of widening inequality, the wealth of those at the top is primarily held in the form of business assets. Research using data from the Survey of Consumer Finances back to the 1940s shows that returns on business equity have been a major source of widening wealth inequality over the decades.7 Most of the income for these wealthy households is in capital income—interest, dividends, capital gains, and business profits—rather than wages and salaries. Unlike these higher-income or wealthier households, most income for middle-class households comes from their paychecks. And it's harder to build wealth that way.

Although the largest asset class for wealthy households is typically stocks and other financial assets, a substantial amount of their capital income also comes from ownership of private businesses. These private businesses come in two broad types: an actively managed business—such as a plumbing business, retail store, or manufacturing firm—and a passive investment in a business, commonly known as private equity. Owning and running a business has long been a way that households become wealthy. But private equity investments are passively run and are typically only open to the wealthiest individuals and families. These private investments have delivered returns to their owners higher than the typical mix of assets held by middle-income households. Private equity investments are 12 percent of the assets of the wealthiest 0.1 percent of households in the most recent Fed survey.

For households above the median but below the 90th percentile, the most prevalent assets are housing and stocks, but the value of housing equity has grown more slowly than that of business equity. About three-fourths of households in this group have stock holdings, mostly through retirement accounts. Although ownership of publicly traded companies has become more widespread across the income spectrum as 401(k) plans have become common over the past few decades, stock ownership by value remains highly concentrated among the wealthiest households. The group of households in the 50th through the 90th percentiles of wealth hold only 17 percent of all public stocks by value, underscoring how much wealth is concentrated.8

Finally, on net, households in the bottom half of the wealth distribution hold just over 2 percent of all wealth, an average of $52,000. About 40 percent of them have equity in homes, and nearly 40 percent own business equity, either through a retirement account or as their own business. But the values are low compared with the upper half of households.

The Evolution of Inequality
By many estimates, today's inequality levels are among the highest in the past 100 years.9 But wealth and income inequality in America have changed significantly over our history, widening and, at times, also narrowing, driven by a variety of factors, including booms and busts in financial assets, structural changes in the economy, and changes in tax and regulatory policy. In the Gilded Age, roughly 1870 to 1910, incomes rose strongly for most people, but it was also the era when large fortunes were accumulated by industrialists and their families. With no income tax or estate tax for the wealthiest and limited public education for everyone, the gap between those at the top and most others widened.10 But over the subsequent decades, especially during the New Deal, legal and institutional change strengthened worker power, and the introduction and broadening of progressive income and estate taxes helped limit the concentration of income and wealth.

Inequality narrowed in the decades after World War II, an era of fast economic expansion that benefited many households. There were improvements in public education, including the GI Bill, income tax changes that made rates more progressive, and growing unionization that raised wages for workers.11

Beginning in the late 1970s, however, wealth and income inequality began to increase again. This was a period of slower economic growth and sometimes high inflation. Top income tax rates declined, and fewer households were required to pay the estate tax. As in the 1910s and 1920s, business equity grew as a share of total assets, helping the wealthiest households become wealthier.12 The wealth generated from the typical middle-class asset—housing—increased but at a slower pace than business assets, exacerbated by the decline in housing wealth during the Global Financial Crisis. Over the same period, real wages grew slowly. Moreover, minimum wage laws failed to keep up with inflation and the growth of the economy.13

The lesson from this history is that the level of inequality in American society is not immutable—it changes over time. These historical patterns suggest that policy choices play a crucial role in shaping inequality levels. Economic growth, technological change, education, and globalization also play key roles.14

The Evolution of the Labor and Capital Shares of National Income
Some of these trends are most clearly seen in the decline in the share of national income going to labor over the past 25 years. Though individuals and families across the spectrum hold business equity from which they receive income, the main source of income for most households is wages and salaries—labor earnings. Since lower- and middle-income households depend heavily on wages, another useful way of exploring income and wealth inequality is to consider how the share of national income earned from labor has changed over time, compared with income earned from capital—dividends, interest, and private business earnings.

After World War II, the labor share of national income was fairly steady between 60 percent and 65 percent, but it began falling in fits and starts beginning in 2001. The decline accelerated after the pandemic and has been especially steep in the past year, reaching a record low of 52.8 percent in the second quarter of 2026.15 Some of this reflects measurement issues associated with how income has been allocated to labor and capital over time.16 But, even after adjusting for those factors, there is a clear downward trend.

Economic research suggests three causes of the decades-long trend in labor versus capital income, each highly relevant to the potential effects of AI on inequality. The first is technological innovations that have reduced demand for labor and decreased wages; the second is the concentration of market power in many industries with a small number of firms; and the third related cause is the reduction in the bargaining power of workers.

Technology replacing labor has been going on for centuries, but new technology can also augment the productivity of existing jobs and create new jobs, including those that are hard to envision when the technology is initially adopted, a point I have noted in a number of speeches.17 The internet and the businesses it created reduced employment in some occupations—such as travel agents—and eliminated others: Think video store workers. But the development of new technologies has expanded jobs in other areas. Consider that there are millions of full-time social media creators earning a living today in the United States, something unimaginable a decade ago.18

What matters for the focus of today's discussion is how returns to labor compare with those to capital from the adoption of new technology. The available evidence from across the globe suggests that advances in digital technology have lowered the price of investment in technology relative to labor. 19 If capital is cheaper relative to labor, firms use relatively more of it. Capital can substitute for labor, too—for example, through the adoption of automation and robots. 20 Some evidence shows that firms that adopt robots may also become more productive and expand their labor force. But in this expansion, labor is outpaced by the expansion in capital, seen in rising profits and equity appreciation, which widens inequality.21 It will be important to watch these developments closely given the immense capital investment going into the AI sector.

Another factor driving the decline in the labor share of national income is market concentration, which researchers estimate has increased significantly in three-quarters of U.S. industries since 2000. Since that time, wages have grown at a slower rate than labor productivity.22 Since larger firms are often more productive, these new technologies, in turn, can contribute to market consolidation, with so-called "superstar" firms as the result.23 Superstar firms also have lower labor shares of income because they can establish higher product markups—the difference between their costs and what they can sell their product for—because there may be less price competition and fewer alternatives for customers.24 At the same time, even with higher markups, their ability to deploy new technologies means these firms may be able to charge lower prices than before, which could serve to further increase their size and may result in lower costs for consumers.

These forces—technological change, market concentration, and declining bargaining power for workers—may have contributed to the declining share of labor income over time.25 All these changes have tended to favor those with the highest incomes and wealth, driving greater wealth inequality.

How Will AI Affect the Labor Share of Income?
The discussion of superstar firms probably brought to mind the handful of companies that are dominating AI: the hyperscalers and frontier AI labs. We have seen tremendous investment in the AI sector, with announced plans suggesting that capital deepening in this sector will escalate further. AI could be what some researchers call capital-biased innovation, in which the returns go primarily to capital rather than to labor.26 In previous remarks, I have explored scenarios for how AI could evolve as a technology and how it could affect labor markets. Key questions include the extent to which AI will substitute for human workers—or largely complement them—and the degree to which AI will create new jobs. Market competition and the bargaining power of workers will also affect the labor share of income. The nature of AI, particularly its ability to learn how to complete complex, nonroutine tasks that require extensive knowledge difficult for humans to achieve, raises the question of whether AI's effects on the labor market will be stronger than other technologies. 27 In previous speeches I have explored whether labor displacement could be larger than in the past, and today I consider the risk that the benefits of this technological revolution could be more narrowly shared than those that came before.28

There are questions also about the concentration of market power. Competition and distributed innovation could make AI a cheap and ubiquitous commodity, with gains widely shared. In this case, most businesses would have access to AI resources sufficient to support improved productivity and further innovation. AI could democratize access to skills and information needed for small businesses to thrive.

But some researchers believe that AI has characteristics that have, in past examples of high-tech advances, reinforced concentration of market power.29 AI depends on access to data, model improvements, and computing power, so it benefits from economies of scale and scope. AI itself is a powerful tool to train and accelerate the development of new AI models, including through self-reinforcement learning.

The extent of the advantage possessed by AI leaders may prove to be unprecedented. If a small number of AI firms dominate the market and receive the lion's share of investment returns, then the wealth of the managers and owners of these firms will surge. Large businesses could afford AI investments and continue to grow, while smaller firms would see declining market share. In this scenario, the vast majority of businesses and their employees would have limited access to ever-improving AI resources, which means they would see smaller returns. Overall, the labor share of income would decline, market concentration would increase, and inequality would go up.

So far, there has been relatively little displacement of workers by AI and some significant evidence that the transition to AI is enhancing the productivity of some workers, increasing demand in some sectors, and even creating new occupations. Vigorous competition among the AI firms so far has meant that access to AI remains fairly broad. Some believe that many businesses will find that most of their AI needs can be fulfilled by smaller-scale language models that will not need to be on the cutting edge of technology. And in that case, benefits would be more broadly shared.

As fast as AI has grown, its implementation is still limited so far, and no one knows the degree to which its benefits will be shared. We are already seeing signs that AI may be a labor substitute in some sectors, especially for younger, less-experienced workers. Some tasks that are easily automated with clear guardrails and predictable outcomes might see rapid labor substitution, while other tasks that require human judgment, management, coordination and relationships, creativity, or outputs that are hard to measure might see more labor augmentation.30 If labor market changes happen quickly, it will be hard for workers to adjust, and dislocations might be large, whereas a more gradual adoption might permit more orderly adjustments.

That said, we should be prepared for the possibility of serious short-term disruptions in the labor market that need to be managed effectively to ensure the benefits of AI are realized over the long term. If AI proves to be capable of work that displaces humans, the extent of the disruption to the labor market will depend in part on whether society undertakes the investments needed in new job creation and worker training, connecting workers to new jobs, and other efforts to mitigate adverse effects for the long term. The concentration of market power may help drive a widening of inequality that would strain the American Dream. In my judgment, now is the time for society to begin to consider how to address these potential challenges—while AI adoption is in its relatively early stages—so we can realize the long-term benefits for society.

November 10, 2026

The U.S. Economy and Considerations for Monetary Policy

Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle W. Bowman

At the C. Peter McColough Series on International Economics Council on Foreign Relations, Washington, D.C.

Share
Watch Live

Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman presented identical remarks at a subsequent event held by Unleash Prosperity Now on the same day.

Good afternoon. It is a pleasure to join you today at the Council on Foreign Relations to discuss my outlook for the U.S. economy.1

Economic growth has gained momentum this year, driven by robust investment demand and solid consumer spending, and the labor market now appears to have stabilized. These outcomes suggest that the policy rate cuts toward the end of last year helped to address some labor market weakness. We are experiencing rapid technological change and ongoing geopolitical tensions, while inflation pressures have persisted for longer than I had hoped and have shown recent signs of broadening. Despite these challenges, increased investment in and use of artificial intelligence (AI), or super intelligence, are boosting economic growth and productivity and American families appear to be resilient.

The Federal Reserve's monetary policy goals are to achieve maximum employment and price stability. While the labor market has improved more than I expected this year, our progress on lowering inflation has disappointed. Therefore, bringing inflation down remains a priority. I am also monitoring expectations about AI and energy prices, and how they influence the risks to achieving our mandates. Consistent with my views following our September FOMC meeting, I continue to focus on interpreting the underlying trends in the economy and the evolution of financial conditions.2

In my remarks today, I will review our most recent FOMC policy meeting and discuss current economic conditions and my near-term outlook for the economy. I will then turn to recent developments in energy markets and the AI buildout and will conclude with my thoughts on the appropriate path of monetary policy.

Update on the Most Recent FOMC Meeting
At last month's FOMC meeting, I joined my colleagues to support holding the federal funds rate target range at 3-3/4 to 4 percent. This approach was consistent with my view that further changes in the policy rate should wait until we had a better understanding of how conditions would evolve and how the recent rate increase would influence the underlying trends in the economy. Maintaining the policy stance at the October meeting allowed us to assess the incoming data. While I continue to expect that several of the factors currently boosting inflation will abate next year, currently inflation has proven to be more persistent than I had anticipated. It is also important to gain greater clarity on the effect of recent market volatility on financial conditions.

Economic and Financial Conditions
U.S. economic growth has stepped up this year. After rising a solid 2.4 percent during the first half of the year, real gross domestic product (GDP) likely increased at an even stronger pace of more than 3 percent in the third quarter, well above last year's 2 percent pace.3 Economic growth has been supported by robust increases in AI-related capital expenditures and solid consumer spending. However, some interest-rate-sensitive sectors like residential investment have declined, and the recent rise in mortgage rates appears to be dragging home construction and sales down further. Consumer spending has strengthened this year, despite softness in real incomes and declining sentiment, which are partly due to higher energy prices.

Labor Market Conditions
Labor market conditions look to have improved since last year. Over the course of this year, the unemployment rate has edged down to 4.1 percent in October, remaining close to my estimate of its natural rate. Job gains have been above last year's anemic pace, averaging about 80,000 per month through October, and have broadened across industries beyond health care and social assistance.

The labor market continues to show low dynamism, with low-hiring and low-firing conditions as we sometimes see in a late-cycle expansion. The job-finding rate has been low, especially for new labor force entrants, and the share of long-term unemployed workers has moved up significantly. Workers' perceptions of job availability in the Conference Board survey have remained soft in recent months. On their own, these data would be consistent with a higher unemployment rate than currently reported. However, layoffs have moved down this year, offsetting the softness in hiring. In plain terms, workers are both less likely to find a new job and less likely to be laid off, a somewhat unusual pattern.

Although stable, the labor market does not appear to be tight, as wage growth has slowed to a modest pace. On a 12-month basis, the wages and salaries component of the employment cost index increased 3.1 percent in September, the same pace as the change in average hourly earnings for October. Both measures have slowed noticeably from a year ago. An increasing number of workers have taken on a supplemental part-time job to make ends meet, as the share of multiple job holders has increased back to levels seen in early 2025. Given strong productivity gains in recent years, wage growth at this pace is in line with or even below the pace we would expect if inflation was running at 2 percent.

Inflation
Progress on lowering inflation has stalled over the past two years or so. Today's consumer price index suggests that 12-month core PCE inflation likely moved up further to 3.3 percent in October, as upward pressures from higher energy prices and AI-related demand continue to flow through to core goods and services prices.

Lack of progress on lowering inflation largely reflects a confluence of shocks unrelated to conditions in the labor market. Tariffs, energy, and AI-related price pressures each accounted for roughly one-fourth of above-target core PCE inflation over the 12 months ending in September. The remaining one-fourth likely reflects persistent effects from the inflationary shocks in recent years. Price pressures had been concentrated in a few categories, but the latest data suggest that these pressures may be broadening.

It is important to ensure that those inflationary pressures do not intensify, further broaden, or become embedded in consumer and business expectations. But today's consumer price index data suggest that inflation pressures may persist for longer. Tariff effects should further wane going forward. Energy-related price pressures should unwind when the Middle East conflict resolves. And inflation pressures related to the AI buildout seem to persist despite some early signs of slowing. I mostly view these pressures as reflecting relative price shocks in specific sectors of the economy. For example, the AI buildout has quickly shifted investment demand toward specific types of high-tech capital, putting upward pressure on prices due to relatively inelastic supply in the near term. As these shocks wane or unwind and with no inflationary pressures emerging in the labor market, my forecast is for core PCE inflation to move closer to 2 percent next year.

Financial Conditions
Financial conditions have tightened a bit recently because of lower equity prices, higher private interest rates, and a stronger dollar. That tightening comes against a backdrop of increased market volatility. Corporate bond and mortgage rate spreads have widened, and equity valuations in the tech sector have dropped, as investors appear to be reassessing expected returns and the risks associated with increased external financing for AI investment projects.

Economic Outlook and Risks
Looking ahead, my expectation is that policies designed to boost the economic potential of the U.S. economy would continue to support productivity, growth, and business confidence. These policies include pragmatic regulations, lower taxes, and more supportive policies toward businesses. I expect the labor market to remain stable and inflation to slow, as special factors currently holding inflation elevated gradually wane or unwind next year. Over time, I also expect increased AI adoption to raise productivity and put some downward pressure on inflation as lower production costs are passed through to consumer prices.

On risks to the outlook, with the labor market improving since last year, I am more attentive to the inflation side of our mandate. Sustained supply-chain pressures from the AI buildout or greater pass-through of higher energy costs could cause inflation pressures to persist. I can also envision a scenario in which the effects of the Middle East conflict and the tech investment boom wane or unwind quickly. In this scenario, I would also expect a timelier return of core PCE inflation toward our target.

I also see a number of risks to the outlook for economic activity, many originating in financial markets. These risks include a large correction in equity valuations, if returns on AI investment fall short of expectations, and a sharp increase in private longer-term interest rates. Lower equity prices could undermine the resilience in consumer spending. And higher interest rates could further strain interest-rate-sensitive sectors of the economy, like housing and small business investment. I will continue to closely monitor developments in credit and equity markets to assess risks to economic growth and financial stability.

Important Issues for Monetary Policy
I see conditions in energy markets and the AI buildout as two important issues for the economy in the months ahead. Developments in these two areas could have material effects on both sides of our mandate and could influence my views on the path for monetary policy.

Energy Markets
The Middle East conflict has disrupted energy markets, impairing the flow of oil and other commodities passing through the Strait of Hormuz, which accounted for about 20 percent of global oil supply before the conflict—roughly equivalent to total U.S. oil consumption. As tensions rose, Brent oil prices moved up to and above $110 per barrel in the second half of October before dropping sharply last week on optimism that an agreement between the U.S. and Iran could be reached following the midterm elections. Although crude oil flows in the Persian Gulf have recovered to near pre-conflict levels in recent weeks, freight rates and insurance premiums have surged because of more complex logistics and ongoing geopolitical risk, raising the cost of delivered oil to many markets around the world.

Prices for gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel also moved up to near or even above their highs in the spring. These higher prices reflected both higher crude oil prices and wider refining margins, as refineries in both the Persian Gulf and Russia have been damaged as a result of ongoing military conflicts in both regions. Prices for refined products have retraced some of the earlier gains more recently as crude oil prices have receded and margins have shown signs of normalizing.

While the outcomes of these conflicts remain uncertain, my approach is to look through temporary energy-driven inflation, when possible, and to remain alert to signs that it is spreading into broader prices or expectations. If geopolitical tensions persist, energy prices may stay elevated in the near term, increasing risks that core inflation may also stay elevated because of increased pass-through to consumer prices of higher production and transportation costs.

Although the U.S. economy is now a net exporter of oil products and less energy intensive, we could still see an impact on economic activity from higher energy prices. The recent deterioration in consumer sentiment measures suggests households may be internalizing a more persistent reduction in their purchasing power. I would be especially concerned about effects on both sides of our mandate if there were further significant disruptions to energy markets. An escalation of geopolitical tensions could have more meaningful effects on real activity because of higher uncertainty and the larger impact of energy prices on lower- and middle-income consumers.

Even with a quick resolution to the Middle East conflict, it may take some time for energy prices to normalize. Futures markets expect oil prices to decline only gradually, as global oil consumption is projected to exceed supply at least through the end of this year. As inventories have been drawn down, the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) has reached a bit less than 50 days of crude oil imports, the lowest level since the early 1980s, reflecting both this year's and the larger 2022 SPR releases. A number of new Gulf pipelines to bypass the Strait of Hormuz are under construction or expansion, with the United Arab Emirates' West–East expansion coming online in early 2027 and most others in 2028 and beyond. Domestic oil production could also continue to rise, which would put downward pressure on fuel costs.

AI Buildout
In remarks earlier this year, I discussed the responsible use of AI in the financial system.4 Today I will describe how this technology is shaping the broader economy. Given the transformative promise of this technology, investment in data centers has boomed and has been a major influence on the U.S. economy. With plans for new data centers continuing to surge, I expect the AI shock to play out over some time. However, as these investments are increasingly being financed with external funds, including off-balance-sheet liabilities, I am also focused on potential risks to the economy and financial stability if revenues from AI customers disappoint.

We may still be in the early days when investment rises rapidly while workers and firms determine the best use cases before we see more meaningful effects on productivity. The boom in data center investment is undeniable, with growth in real business fixed investment strengthening further this year to a pace like the 1990s tech boom. High-tech equipment, software, and data center construction have accounted for the large majority of growth in business investment since 2025, with gains broadening to other categories this year.

AI adoption has grown significantly, especially among larger firms, with about 25 percent of businesses and roughly 10 percent of workers reporting using AI every day in their work. However, use varies greatly across industries and occupations, with stronger adoption in information and other business services sectors. I think there is a risk that some workers will be displaced as firms adopt this technology, even as others see their productivity—and wages—rise. Monetary policy cannot address that transition directly, but it should support a dynamic labor market that facilitates the necessary retraining and mobility of workers across jobs.

AI revenues have been rising quickly but are not yet sufficient to pay for the expense of maintaining existing and developing new AI models and data centers. As capital spending has surged, hyperscalers and other companies have increasingly been sourcing funds from credit and equity markets. And as investors see an increased risk in these investments, the cost of financing has been rising notably.

In addition to financing costs, the AI buildout is also putting upward pressure on a number of price categories. Prices of memory chips, semiconductors, computers, and other electronic products have increased sharply this year—in a few categories by double digits—as demand from data centers has outpaced supply. I expect production capacity to catch up to demand gradually over time.

AI-related debt has been rising rapidly, with notable gains in corporate bonds, private credit, and project finance, and is on track to exceed $800 billion this year. Other obligations, in the form of long-term lease and contract commitments and residual value guarantees, have also surged and their potential payments are estimated at more than three times the amount of debt. Given their strong credit ratings, hyperscalers and large tech firms have assumed off-balance-sheet liabilities to allow smaller and riskier companies to use them as collateral in asset-based financing for data center construction and the purchase of accelerated computing equipment. These connections among AI firms have also been accompanied by hyperscalers and chip designers making equity investments in smaller companies that purchase their products, creating the appearance of circular financing.

The largest U.S. bank holding companies have some exposure to AI-related financing, which is scrutinized during annual supervisory stress tests. Life insurance companies, pension funds, private credit, and mutual funds also have exposure. Most of these entities are well capitalized and have stable funding. But the complex nature of the financing structures makes it difficult to properly assess counterparty risk.

Risks seem to be concentrated in only a few large firms that provide AI services, including AI labs and the hyperscalers. If AI-related revenues fall short of sharply rising costs, especially as public sentiment and local opposition could lead to increased regulation and delays, we could see a sharp correction in equity prices for AI firms, higher financing costs, and reduced access to credit. This shock would likely slow the AI buildout and soften both economic activity and inflation.

We may have already seen some reassessment of expected returns play out in recent weeks as equity valuations dropped, corporate bond spreads widened, and credit default swaps increased for some companies involved in the AI buildout. I will be looking at how momentum in adoption of and investment in this technology evolves and for possible effects on both inflation and employment.

The Path Forward for Monetary Policy
As we think about the path ahead, it is important to remember that over the past two years the U.S. economy has experienced successive inflationary shocks that have not been driven by labor market conditions. Monetary policy cannot offset the direct effects of tariffs and energy costs on inflation, but it plays a central role in containing the second-round effects. Monetary policy also has limited influence on AI-related price pressures. However, in certain segments of AI-intensive sectors, particularly among newer and medium-sized firms, higher interest rates appear to be holding back investment and purchasing decisions.

As I noted earlier, financial conditions have tightened. Against this backdrop, the benefit of additional policy tightening has to be carefully weighed against its costs for interest-rate-sensitive sectors. The October Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey on Bank Lending Practices (SLOOS) suggests that the concentration of demand weakness in real estate loan categories reflects existing headwinds in those sectors.5

Time will help us learn more about the durability of momentum in AI-related demand and the evolution of geopolitical risks, both of which could be critical in the months ahead for setting our policy stance. Greater clarity on AI-related financing conditions and the sustainability of AI investments should increase my confidence in future policy decisions. Geopolitical conditions affect commodity markets and the degree of economic uncertainty perceived by households and businesses.

Before our next meeting in December, we will receive the PCE inflation report for October and the employment report for November. Today, I am seeing signs that inflation pressures are persisting, while labor market conditions appear to be firming. This indicates that there is still work to do to bring inflation closer to 2 percent. Given the recent market volatility, I will also continue to closely monitor how developments in financial conditions influence the economic outlook.

In the meantime, I will continue to meet with a broad range of contacts to inform my assessment of economic conditions and the appropriate stance of policy. It is important to remember that monetary policy is not on a preset course. At each FOMC meeting, my colleagues and I will evaluate incoming data, the evolving outlook, and the implications for the risks to our dual-mandate goals of maximum employment and price stability.

Thank you again for the invitation to join you this afternoon. I look forward to our discussion.

The Economic Outlook and Some Thoughts on Labor Market Dynamism

Governor Christopher J. Waller

At the Bank of England Watchers Conference King’s Business School and Money, Macro and Finance Society, London, United Kingdom

Share
Watch Live

Thank you, Paul, and welcome to Bank of England watchers, who I suspect are watching the Federal Reserve as well.1

My subject today is the outlook for the U.S. economy and the implications for monetary policy. I think there is little disagreement about the basics of that outlook. The U.S. economy is growing strongly, with a labor market that has brought employment close to its long-term rate. But inflation remains too high, so I will explain why I believe further tightening of monetary policy will be needed, sooner rather than later, to return inflation to a path that will reach 2 percent in the medium term.

The outline of my speech is to first explain why I voted to hold the policy rate steady at the most recent meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC). Then I will talk about the general state of the U.S. economy. After that, I will ask some open-ended questions about the dynamism of the U.S. labor market that are important to consider over the longer run. I'll follow that by a discussion of recent inflation data and then describe my views about the future path of monetary policy.

At our most recent meeting, I supported the FOMC majority in holding monetary policy at its current setting. As I said in a speech a couple weeks before then, my rationale was that the Committee's communication about the likely tightening of policy had already led financial markets to price in some tightening.2 When this communication is clear and credible, what really matters is that those hikes are eventually carried out—it is not necessary, for example, to raise rates at consecutive meetings.

If, however, the hikes are not carried out, markets will reduce expectations of rate hikes and rates will immediately fall, which would loosen monetary policy in an undesirable way. So, while the precise timing of the hikes doesn't matter, ensuring that the promised hikes are implemented in a reasonable period of time does.

Let me turn to the economy. Economic output continues to grow at a healthy pace. The initial estimate was that real gross domestic product (GDP) rose 3.4 percent in the third quarter, supported by strong consumer spending and robust investment. Consumption growth was helped by a booming stock market, and investment by businesses continued to surge, especially in high-tech equipment associated with the artificial intelligence (AI) buildout. I expect GDP growth is backing off a bit in the current quarter, as consumer spending slows to levels that I think are more consistent with household income and wealth, but output will continue to grow at a solid pace.

The labor market has also been solid. The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.1 percent in October, the same rate it was over the summer. Job creation, which has been fluctuating widely month to month for some time, saw October gains of 165,000 jobs and 85,000 jobs added to estimates of the previous two months. To look through the volatility, the 6-month and 12-month average payroll gains have been closer to 80,000 jobs a month. This level is a bit above most estimates of the breakeven level needed to keep the unemployment rate steady but entirely consistent with my view that the supply and demand for labor are in rough balance.

While the labor market has been stable, for over a year now it has been what is best characterized as "low hire, low fire." One can understand this characterization by looking at job separation and hiring rates today and comparing them with what they were before the pandemic (see figures 1 and 2). You will see that the rate of separations, the sum of layoffs and quits, has been persistently low the past few years compared with a decade ago. The hiring rate is also low. This is unusual because, in a strong labor market, the hiring rate is usually high. Hiring tends to be low when the labor market is weak, which is when the firing rate is often elevated. What is unusual today, and may be unprecedented, is the extent to which both firing and hiring are now so low.

These data have led me to consider a question that has longer-term implications for the economy and monetary policy: Does a stable labor market necessarily mean it's a healthy labor market? To illustrate the difference, if your mom was in the hospital and the doctor told you her condition was stable, you would be relieved but you wouldn't say she is healthy. I am concerned that the low hire, low fire equilibrium in the labor market means it has lost some of its dynamism, which is important for productivity growth and real wages. If the labor market is in fact showing less dynamism, is it an issue for monetary policy? While I don't have answers to these questions, I do think the questions are worth asking.

This discussion starts with distinguishing between net employment growth and gross flows in and out of employment. Behind the monthly job creation numbers that get most of the world's attention is the enormous churn in the labor market. For example, the 60,000 jobs created in September was a net change in the level of employment, but it is dwarfed by the more than 5 million people who left their jobs that month and the more than 5 million who were hired. Despite the low separation and hiring rates, the level of separations and hiring is huge. When I was a professor, I would use the following analogy for my students. If you stand by a river, you can measure the water level. If you return the next day and measure again, you will likely find that the water level is unchanged—it is stable. But in fact, a massive amount of water has flowed downstream since the day before. The same goes for the labor market—there is a lot of churning even if the level of employment is stable.

The question is, does churn in the labor market matter? If so, is labor market churn bad or good? One could argue that this churn is inefficient. Why are people and firms wasting time swapping workers across jobs? If workers simply stayed at their firms, and firms never let them go, employment would be stable and resources would be saved from putting workers into new jobs. But would it mean the labor market is healthy?

The answer is that the churn in hiring and firing is actually good for the economy. This churn reflects a dynamic market that allows labor resources to be allocated to more productive uses. If a worker and firm are not in a good, productive match, then it is more efficient and better for the economy for the worker and firm to separate and look for new partners. When more productive matches are formed, productivity, real wages, and profits will be higher. In this situation, employment may not be stable, but it is healthier.

I believe this churn is what leads to the exceptional performance of the U.S. economy. The relative ease of starting businesses in the United States, compared with many other advanced economies, is a big advantage. The relative ease of firing employees, compared with many European Union economies, is surely not welcome by the workers affected, but cumulatively it was a factor that drove the stronger recovery in the United States from the pandemic recession. This flexibility in hiring and firing promotes a better match between skills of the worker and those demanded by businesses, making the most productive use of their abilities. As a result, this churn boosts the capacity of the economy and allows incomes to grow without inflationary pressure.

So, given this discussion, how do we interpret the current low hire, low fire economy? Does it indicate that the U.S. labor market has become less dynamic and less healthy, which has implications for the economic outlook and for monetary policy? I will first consider the possible causes of this lower dynamism and then the possible implications.

One explanation for the lower dynamism is that some of the decline in both hiring and firing may reflect, in part, a continuation of longer-run trends that were in place even before the pandemic. First, it is well known that the workforce is aging for a number of reasons: People are living longer and are healthier and more active at older ages; many members of the large post–World War II baby boom are in their 60s and 70s; and birth rates have declined, which increases the relative age of the workforce. This aging affects the dynamism of the labor market because older workers switch jobs less frequently, since many have managed over time to find better job matches and because older workers are less likely to relocate for work. The second trend is that the level of education of the workforce is increasing over time. More educated workers experience less job churn in general and, during downturns, face lower rates of job separations. Since 2010, the share of adults 25 and older with at least a bachelor's degree has increased from 28 percent to 37 percent.3

If these were the only reasons for the decline in labor market dynamism, then it would be at a level consistent with these trend factors, suggesting an appropriate degree of dynamism with no implications for monetary policy. But the data suggest that, even after accounting for these factors, labor market dynamism still seems somewhat low. There are several possible reasons.

One is that hiring and job-switching may have been restrained by the confluence of factors that have raised economic uncertainty since early 2025: the advent of AI, tariffs, changes in immigration policy and enforcement, and a general level of uncertainty about the economic outlook that has made business planning particularly challenging. One piece of evidence was a special question posed in the Federal Reserve's November 2025 Beige Book survey of economic conditions: Employers reported in November 2025 that hiring was unusually restrained because of uncertainty. I don't think uncertainty has diminished much since then. And another piece of evidence is in the most recent Beige Book, showing that several Fed Districts reported firms had delayed hiring or anticipate less job creation after investing in AI.

Another reason for low dynamism is that low firing may be a consequence of the huge surge in job-switching from 2020 to 2023 related to the pandemic. Workers in new jobs are less inclined to change jobs again, and that may be limiting job-switching. If this is a large factor, it may be temporary—workers are in their jobs for a median of three years before switching again, and we are nearing that threshold for the last of the pandemic job switchers. But it's possible they could be staying longer now if the job-switching triggered by the pandemic had been unusually effective at improving the matches between workers and jobs—many relocated or chased dreams and new careers and may not be as willing to switch again.

As you can tell, it is hard to pin down and properly weigh the possible causes of lower labor market dynamism and then judge how long that phenomenon might continue. This makes it hard to know the implications for the economic outlook and monetary policy.

But we can talk more constructively, I think, about the possible effects of the current low hire, low fire environment. While low layoffs imply that fewer workers experience unemployment, a low hiring rate means that those who do become unemployed may experience longer unemployment spells. And, in fact, the percentage of unemployed people who have been looking for work for more than six months has climbed steadily since 2022 and is now quite elevated for a period of economic expansion.4 Another logical consequence of lower dynamism in the labor market would be that new entrants, typically young and with less experience, are having a harder time finding jobs. I think anyone paying attention to the U.S. economy has been hearing about the challenges young workers are facing. Indeed, the share of new labor force entrants in unemployment has been rising and has reached close to the highest levels of the past twenty years.

Both these developments may reflect a labor market that is not as healthy as the headline unemployment rate indicates, with implications for the longer-term path of monetary policy. If hires and separations are low because worker matches have improved, then this may be a boost to productivity and can be largely ignored in assessing the state of the labor market. But I worry that today's labor market dynamism may be low due to impediments to mobility that are affecting long-term and youth unemployment, and this may lead to a less-efficient distribution of workers across jobs, hindering productivity growth. This possibility is important to monitor because productivity growth is a crucial factor in judging how fast the economy can grow without undesirable inflationary pressure. As I said earlier, I don't have the answers to the questions I have posed, but I believe the questions are worth asking and addressing with more research.

Let me put aside this discussion about the health of the labor market for now. What is important in the near term is that I see the labor market as stable and employment at a level close to the FOMC's maximum-employment goal.

By contrast, inflation is well above 2 percent and the focus of my attention. For reasons I explained at the start of this speech, I was willing to wait to tighten monetary policy at the FOMC's October meeting. Inconveniently for today's event, we will get consumer price index (CPI) inflation for October in a few hours. While I am hoping for an improvement over September, even if this occurs, I would still support a 25 basis point increase in the FOMC's policy rate at our December meeting. However, if inflation comes in substantially higher than I expect, more robust action by the FOMC may be called for.

Let's just say it. September's inflation data were bad. There was no silver lining. Inflation based on personal consumption expenditures (PCE), the measure targeted by the FOMC, rose at a monthly rate of 0.5 percent in September and 3.7 percent over the previous 12 months. Core PCE inflation, excluding food and energy prices, rose 0.3 percent in September and 3.1 percent from that time last year. Both these measures are too high, and I am particularly concerned about the breadth of price increases. Earlier this year, excessive inflation was driven by nonmarket prices that are estimated rather than observed by the government, and ones that I tend to discount as hard to measure and as an unreliable guide to the overall trend for core inflation. But now, two components with greater relevance to future inflation are elevated—core goods prices and those for core market-based services excluding housing. More than 70 percent of the components of core PCE service prices saw 12-month increases above 3 percent in September.

Later today, I will be looking to see whether this breadth of elevated inflation continues, including whether high-tech goods prices related to the AI buildout continue to rise at the very high rate of recent months. I also want to know if the run-up in energy prices of the past several months is being passed through to goods and services directly or indirectly, because of higher input costs. While I am pleased to see energy prices down from their high rates over the past week or so, they are still quite elevated relative to the start of the year, and the ultimate effect on inflation will depend on what levels are sustained.

Federal Reserve staff have been thinking hard about the effects on inflation of the AI buildout and elevated energy prices, and their findings do not encourage me to think that these factors will be fading anytime soon.

In one exercise, researchers tried to capture imminent price pressures with a textual analysis of the well-known surveys of purchasing managers conducted by the Institute for Supply Management (ISM).5 ISM surveys ask purchasing managers to list specific commodities, materials, and services whose prices rose or fell that month.6 Staff used these responses to construct monthly price pressure indexes that correspond with actual categories of the producer price index (PPI). They found these indexes provide information about future changes in producer prices and, as a result, should have some signal about future PCE inflation. For example, the index for high-tech products rose in December 2025 and jumped further in January 2026, ahead of an escalation in those goods prices that eventually showed up in the February PPI and inflation data. The most recent ISM data, for October, point to the possibility of more price increases ahead.

Some other Fed research indicates to me that energy price pressures may pass through to goods and services in a more sustained way than in the past.7 The researchers find a potential shift in the inflation process after the pandemic that suggests core inflation may be more responsive to energy shocks. Central bankers have tended to look through energy price shocks and expect that their effects on inflation will soon fade, but this new work cautions against taking too much comfort in this view. Their analysis suggests that, especially if oil prices were to remain elevated, these pressures could be larger than previously though and persist well into 2027.

And there are reasons to also expect elevated energy prices to persist. First, even as there is increased tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, that traffic is moving at great expense. The insurance now needed for ships making this passing, and the ship-to-ship transfer of oil necessary to guard against Iranian attacks, are reportedly boosting oil prices by $20 to $30 a barrel. A second problem is that diesel oil production is affected by limited refining capacity and other constraints. As a consequence, elevated prices for diesel in the United States are not expected to moderate as readily as crude oil prices would in a more peaceful Middle East.

To recap my economic outlook, economic activity is strong, the labor market is stable, but inflation is too high, well above the FOMC's 2 percent goal. This means that even if, as I expect, the monthly rate of inflation improves in October, I am leaning toward supporting an increase in our policy rate of 25 basis points at the FOMC's December meeting.

That's my plan if things proceed as I expect. But if the October CPI inflation data out later today are similar to the past two monthly readings—with core CPI inflation around 0.3 percent or even higher—then a stronger policy move may be needed. In that case, I believe a 50 basis point hike in the policy rate should be considered at the December meeting. Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take.

National Summary
Overall Economic Activity

Since the prior Beige Book, two of the twelve Federal Reserve Districts reported moderate growth in economic activity. Three Districts reported modest growth, while four reported slight growth, and three reported no significant change. Robust, broad-based growth in manufacturing led the moderate growth reported in the Dallas District. While not as robust, another six Districts reported modest to moderate growth from their manufacturing firms. The remaining five Districts noted little or no manufacturing growth. Reports on other sectors indicate little growth overall. Most Districts reported little or no change in overall consumer spending. Auto contacts noted flat to modestly lower sales in five of the six Districts that reported. Over half of the Districts noted that consumer spending was driven by high-income households. Even in Dallas, where District firms reported solid retail sales, there were "some reports of lower-income customers being more cautious with spending." Tourism continued to grow, but firms in the Boston, Minneapolis, and San Francisco Districts noted a decline in sales from Canadian tourists. Overall, most Districts reported that firms have maintained a positive economic outlook for the coming months. However, expectations tend to be for slight growth, weighted toward manufacturing and burdened by persistent uncertainties.

Labor Markets

Most Districts described little or no net change in overall employment levels. While three Districts described modest increases, five reported slight increases, and four reported no significant change. Overall, labor market conditions were little changed. Labor supply was generally adequate to meet demand; however, shortages persisted for specialized, technical, and skilled trade workers. Five Districts reported that some firms had either reduced staffing levels (through attrition), delayed hiring, or anticipated less job creation after increasing productivity by investing in AI. Five Districts described moderate growth in overall wages, four reported modest growth, and two reported slight growth. Several Districts noted that inflation has spawned additional wage pressure in the form of higher cost-of-living demands from individuals as well as challenges from rising benefit costs. Staffing contacts in the Boston District noted that workers were motivated to seek new jobs as wage increases lagged inflation.

Prices

Three Districts characterized overall price growth as robust, while eight noted that growth was moderate—and three of the eight noted that input price pressures were intensifying, if not already growing robustly. One District reported modest price growth. Moreover, four Districts reported that the pace of price increases picked up since the prior period. Higher oil prices permeated all District reports. Firms reported elevated diesel prices and noted that fuel surcharges were passing on increased costs throughout the supply chain. Contacts in multiple Districts also noted continued price pressure from other commodities, tariffs, and insurance. Consumer-facing firms noted tighter profit margins as price-sensitive customers continue to resist price hikes. Contacts are managing their margins with selective price hikes, targeted incentives, and menu changes that reduce their costs. Firms in several Districts cited persistent uncertainty around energy and tariffs and expect that prices will increase further in the coming months, as existing contracts expire, and firms must renegotiate hedge contracts for their own inputs and/or delivery contracts to their customers.

                            THE EMPLOYMENT SITUATION - OCTOBER 2026


Total nonfarm payroll employment rose by 165,000 in October, and the unemployment rate changed 
little at 4.1 percent, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported today. Employment increased in 
professional and business services, in health care, and in transportation and warehousing.

This news release presents statistics from two monthly surveys. The household survey measures labor 
force status, including unemployment, by demographic characteristics. The establishment survey 
measures nonfarm employment, hours, and earnings by industry. For more information about the 
concepts and statistical methodology used in these two surveys, see the Technical Note.

Household Survey Data

Both the unemployment rate, at 4.1 percent, and the number of unemployed people, at 7.1 million, 
changed little in October. The unemployment rate has remained in a narrow range of 4.1 percent to 
4.3 percent since March. (See table A-1.)

Among the major worker groups, the unemployment rates showed little or no change in October for 
adult men (3.9 percent), adult women (3.6 percent), teenagers (13.9 percent), and people who are 
White (3.7 percent), Black (6.6 percent), Asian (2.8 percent), or Hispanic (4.7 percent). (See

The number of long-term unemployed (those jobless for 27 weeks or more), at 2.0 million, changed 
little over the month. The long-term unemployed accounted for 27.6 percent of all unemployed 
people. (See table A-12.)

Both the labor force participation rate, at 61.9 percent, and the employment-population ratio, at 
59.3 percent, changed little in October. These measures have shown little net change since January.

The number of people employed part time for economic reasons increased by 339,000 to 4.8 million in 
October. These individuals would have preferred full-time employment but were working part time

Technical Note

Brief Explanation of the CPI
The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures the change in prices paid by consumers for goods and services. The CPI reflects
spending patterns for each of two population groups: all urban consumers and urban wage earners and clerical workers.
The all urban consumer group represents over 90 percent of the total U.S. population. It is based on the expenditures
of almost all residents of urban or metropolitan areas, including professionals, the self-employed, the poor, the
unemployed, and retired people, as well as urban wage earners and clerical workers. Not included in the CPI are the
spending patterns of people living in rural nonmetropolitan areas, farming families, people in the Armed Forces, and
those in institutions, such as prisons and mental hospitals. Consumer inflation for all urban consumers is measured by
two indexes, namely, the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) and the Chained Consumer Price Index for
All Urban Consumers (C-CPI-U). 

The Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W) is based on the expenditures of households
included in the CPI-U definition that meet two requirements: more than one-half of the household's income must come
from clerical or wage occupations, and at least one of the household's earners must have been employed for at least 37
weeks during the previous 12 months. The CPI-W population represents approximately 30 percent of the total U.S.
population and is a subset of the CPI-U population.

The CPIs are based on prices of food, clothing, shelter, fuels, transportation, doctors' and dentists' services, drugs,
and other goods and services that people buy for day-to-day living. Prices are collected each month in 75 urban areas
across the country from about 6,000 housing units and approximately 22,000 retail establishments (department stores,
supermarkets, hospitals, and other types of stores and service establishments). All taxes directly associated with the
purchase and use of items are included in the index. Prices of fuels and a few other items are obtained every month in
all 75 locations. Prices of most other commodities and services are collected every month in the three largest
geographic areas and every other month in other areas. Prices of most goods and services are obtained by personal
visit, telephone call, web, or app collection by the Bureau's trained representatives.

In calculating the index, price changes for the various items in each location are aggregated using weights, which
represent their importance in the spending of the appropriate population group. Local data are then combined to obtain
a U.S. city average. For the CPI-U and CPI-W, separate indexes are also published by size of city, by region of the
country, for cross-classifications of regions and population-size classes, and for 23 selected local areas. Area
indexes do not measure differences in the level of prices among cities; they only measure the average change in prices
for each area since the base period. For the C-CPI-U, data are issued only at the national level. The CPI-U and CPI-W
are considered final when released, but the C-CPI-U is issued in preliminary form and subject to three subsequent
quarterly revisions. 

The index measures price change from a designed reference date. For most of the CPI-U and the CPI-W, the reference base
is 1982-84 equals 100. The reference base for the C-CPI-U is December 1999 equals 100. An increase of 7 percent from
the reference base, for example, is shown as 107.000. Alternatively, that relationship can also be expressed as the
price of a base period market basket of goods and services rising from $100 to $107. 

Sampling Error in the CPI

The CPI is a statistical estimate that is subject to sampling error because it is based upon a sample of retail prices
and not the complete universe of all prices. BLS calculates and publishes estimates of the 1-month, 2-month, 6-month,
and 12-month percent change standard errors annually for the CPI-U. These standard error estimates can be used to
construct confidence intervals for hypothesis testing. For example, the estimated standard error of the 1-month percent
change is 0.04 percent for the U.S. all items CPI. This means that if we repeatedly sample from the universe of all
retail prices using the same methodology, and estimate a percentage change for each sample, then 95 percent of these
estimates will be within 0.08 percent of the 1-month percentage change based on all retail prices. For example, for a
1-month change of 0.2 percent in the all items CPI-U, we are 95 percent confident that the actual percent change based
on all retail prices would fall between 0.12 and 0.28 percent. For the latest data, including information on how to use
the estimates of standard error, see www.bls.gov/cpi/tables/variance-estimates/home.htm. 

Calculating Index Changes

Movements of the indexes from 1 month to another are usually expressed as percent changes rather than changes in index
points, because index point changes are affected by the level of the index in relation to its base period, while
percent changes are not. The following table shows an example of using index values to calculate percent changes:

                            Item A                  Item B                      Item C
Year I                      112.500                 225.000                     110.000
Year II                     121.500                 243.000                     128.000
Change in index points      9.000                   18.000                      18.000
Percent change              9.0/112.500 x 100 = 8.0  18.0/225.000 x 100 = 8.0   18.0/110.000 x 100 = 16.4

Use of Seasonally Adjusted and Unadjusted Data

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) program produces both unadjusted and seasonally adjusted data. Seasonally adjusted data
are computed using seasonal factors derived by the X-13ARIMA-SEATS seasonal adjustment method. These factors are
updated each February, and the new factors are used to revise the previous 5 years of seasonally adjusted data. The
factors are available at www.bls.gov/web/cpi/cpi-seasonal-factors.xlsx. For more information on data revision
scheduling, please see the Seasonal Adjustment questions and answers page at
www.bls.gov/cpi/seasonal-adjustment/questions-and-answers.htm and the Timeline of Seasonal Adjustment Methodological
Changes at www.bls.gov/cpi/seasonal-adjustment/timeline-seasonal-adjustment-methodology-changes.htm. 

How to Use Seasonally Adjusted and Unadjusted Data

For analyzing short-term price trends in the economy, seasonally adjusted changes are usually preferred since they
eliminate the effect of changes that normally occur at the same time and in about the same magnitude every year-such as
price movements resulting from weather events, production cycles, model changeovers, holidays, and sales. This allows
data users to focus on changes that are not typical for the time of year. 

The unadjusted data are of primary interest to consumers concerned about the prices they actually pay. Unadjusted data
are also used extensively for escalation purposes. Many collective bargaining contract agreements and pension plans,
for example, tie compensation changes to the Consumer Price Index before adjustment for seasonal variation. BLS advises
against the use of seasonally adjusted data in escalation agreements because seasonally adjusted series are revised
annually for five years.

Intervention Analysis

The Bureau of Labor Statistics uses intervention analysis seasonal adjustment (IASA) for some CPI series. Sometimes
extreme values or sharp movements can distort the underlying seasonal pattern of price change. Intervention analysis
seasonal adjustment is a process by which the distortions caused by such unusual events are estimated and removed from
the data prior to calculation of seasonal factors. The resulting seasonal factors, which more accurately represent the
seasonal pattern, are then applied to the unadjusted data.

For example, this procedure was used for the motor fuel series to offset the effects of the 2009 return to normal
pricing after the worldwide economic downturn in 2008. Retaining this outlier data during seasonal factor calculation
would distort the computation of the seasonal portion of the time series data for motor fuel, so it was estimated and
removed from the data prior to seasonal adjustment. Following that, seasonal factors were calculated based on this
"prior adjusted" data. These seasonal factors represent a clearer picture of the seasonal pattern in the data. The last
step is for motor fuel seasonal factors to be applied to the unadjusted data.

For the seasonal factors introduced for January 2026, BLS adjusted 57 series using intervention analysis seasonal
adjustment, including selected food and beverage items, motor fuels and vehicles.

Revision of Seasonally Adjusted Indexes

Seasonally adjusted data, including the U.S. city average all items index levels, are subject to revision for up to 5
years after their original release. Every year, economists in the CPI calculate new seasonal factors for seasonally
adjusted series and apply them to the last 5 years of data. Seasonally adjusted indexes beyond the last 5 years of
data are considered to be final and not subject to revision. For January 2026, revised seasonal factors and seasonally
adjusted indexes for 2021 to 2025 were calculated and published. For series which are directly adjusted using the
Census X-13ARIMA-SEATS seasonal adjustment software, the seasonal factors for 2025 will be applied to data for 2026 to
produce the seasonally adjusted 2026 indexes. Series which are indirectly seasonally adjusted by summing seasonally
adjusted component series have seasonal factors which are derived and are therefore not available in advance.

Determining Seasonal Status

Each year the seasonal status of every series is reevaluated based upon certain statistical criteria. Using these
criteria, BLS economists determine whether a series should change its status from "not seasonally adjusted" to
"seasonally adjusted", or vice versa. If any of the 81 components of the U.S. city average all items index change
their seasonal adjustment status from seasonally adjusted to not seasonally adjusted, not seasonally adjusted data
will be used in the aggregation of the dependent series for the last 5 years, but the seasonally adjusted indexes
before that period will not be changed. For 2026, 36 of the 81 components of the U.S. city average all items index are
not seasonally adjusted.

Personal Income and Outlays, September 2026

Personal income increased $54.5 billion (0.2 percent at a monthly rate) in September, according to estimates released today by the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). Disposable personal income (DPI)—personal income less personal current taxes—increased $52.7 billion (0.2 percent), and personal consumption expenditures (PCE) increased $113.1 billion (0.5 percent).

Personal outlays—the sum of PCE, personal interest payments, and personal current transfer payments—increased $113.0 billion in September. Personal saving was $911.1 billion, and the personal saving rate—personal saving as a percentage of DPI—was 3.8 percent.

The increase in current-dollar personal income in September primarily reflected increases in compensation and government social benefits.

The $113.1 billion increase in current-dollar PCE in September reflected increases of $35.1 billion in spending on goods and $78.1 billion in spending on services.

Real PCE increased $2.6 billion (less than 0.1 percent at a monthly rate) in September.

From the preceding month, the PCE price index for September increased 0.5 percent. Excluding food and energy, the PCE price index increased 0.3 percent.

From the same month one year ago, the PCE price index for September increased 3.6 percent. Excluding food and energy, the PCE price index increased 3.1 percent from one year ago.

GDP (Advance Estimate), 3rd Quarter 2026

Real gross domestic product (GDP) increased at an annual rate of 3.4 percent in the third quarter of 2026 (July, August, and September), according to the advance estimate released today by the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). In the second quarter, real GDP increased 2.2 percent.

The contributors to the increase in real GDP in the third quarter were increases in investment, consumer spending, and government spending that were partly offset by a decrease in exports. Imports, which are a subtraction in the calculation of GDP, increased. For more information, refer to the "Technical Notes" below.

Compared to the second quarter, the acceleration in real GDP in the third quarter primarily reflected an acceleration in investment and an upturn in government spending that were partly offset by a downturn in exports. Imports increased more in the third quarter than in the second quarter.

Real final sales to private domestic purchasers, the sum of consumer spending and gross private fixed investment, increased 4.9 percent in the third quarter, compared with an increase of 4.6 percent in the second quarter.

The price index for gross domestic purchases increased 3.0 percent in the third quarter, compared with an increase of 5.6 percent in the second quarter. The personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index increased 2.1 percent, compared with an increase of 5.0 percent, and the PCE price index, excluding food and energy increased 2.5 percent, compared with an increase of 3.3 percent.

Technical Notes
Sources of change for real GDP

Real GDP increased at an annual rate of 3.4 percent (0.8 percent at a quarterly rate 1) in the third quarter, reflecting increases in investment, consumer spending, and government spending that were partly offset by a decrease in exports. Imports, which are a subtraction in the calculation of GDP, increased.

More information on the source data and BEA assumptions that underlie the third-quarter estimate is shown in the key source data and assumptions table.

The increase in investment primarily reflected increases in private inventory investment and in equipment.
The largest contributors to the increase in private inventory investment were wholesale trade and manufacturing (both led by nondurable goods industries), based primarily on Census Bureau inventory book value data.
Within equipment, the increase was led by information processing equipment (mainly computers and peripheral equipment), based primarily on data for imports from the U.S. Census Bureau-BEA U.S. International Trade in Goods and Services report and the Census Bureau Advance Economic Indicators Report for September.
The increase in consumer spending reflected increases in both services and goods.
Within services, the increase was led by health care (mainly hospitals and outpatient services), based primarily on U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Current Employment Statistics, and food services and accommodations (mainly purchased meals and beverages), based on Census Bureau Monthly Retail Trade Survey (MRTS) data.
Within goods, the increase was led by other nondurable goods (mainly prescription drugs), based on Census Bureau MRTS data.
Within government spending, the increase was more than accounted for by federal government spending, led by nondefense consumption expenditures. The pattern of nondefense spending primarily reflected sales of crude oil from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, based on data from the U.S. Department of Energy. Within the National Economic Accounts, sales are deducted from government consumption expenditures. In the third quarter, sales from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve were less than sales in the second quarter, resulting in an increase in consumption expenditures. Because the oil sold by the government is reflected as an increase in other components of GDP, there is no direct effect on GDP.
Exports and imports primarily reflected Census Bureau-BEA U.S. International Trade in Goods and Services data as well as the Census Bureau Advance Economic Indicators Report for September. The decrease in exports primarily reflected a decrease in goods (led by petroleum and related products). The largest contributor to imports was capital goods, except automotive.
