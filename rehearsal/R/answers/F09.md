# F09 - FOMC decision, 8-9 December 2026

Source: evidence pack /home/claude/cp/R/pack.md only (21 official documents, captured 2026-11-23). Facts are taken from the pack; items marked "inference" are my own reading.

## 1. What the Committee did recently and what it signals for December

Recent actions
- September 15-16: raised the target range 25 bp to 3-3/4 to 4 percent ("removed a dose of accommodation"). The September SEP put the median end-2026 funds rate at 4.1 percent: 12 of 18 participants at 4.125 (one more 25 bp hike), 4 at 4.375 (two more), 2 at 3.875 (no more). December is the only meeting left in 2026, so 16 of 18 dots required at least one more hike by this meeting.
- October 27-28: held at 3-3/4 to 4 percent by a 10-2 vote. Cook and Hammack dissented in favour of a 25 bp hike. The upper bound in effect going into December is therefore 4.00 percent.

Signals from the October minutes (published 18 November)
- "Almost all participants" judged that further action to raise the target range, broadly consistent with the September SEP, would likely be appropriate.
- "Some participants" said market-implied tightening steps in December and next year were broadly consistent with their view of the appropriate path, and "several" saw that alignment as what made holding in October low-risk. Inference: the October hold was explicitly leaning on a priced-in December hike, which raises the cost of not delivering it.
- "Some participants" wanted to hike already in October; "a few" holders called it a close call; "a couple" said more than 25 bp could warrant consideration at the next meeting if inflation data were unfavourable.
- Market pricing at the October meeting: high probability of a 25 bp hike by year-end; Desk survey median expected a December hike.
- Counterweights in the minutes: a majority noted tighter financial conditions since September; "a couple" favoured a gradual approach; "many" wanted to assess the durability of AI investment demand, financing conditions and geopolitics.

Signals from members since the meeting
- Waller (10 Nov, before the CPI release): would support 25 bp in December even if October inflation improved; said that if core CPI came in "around 0.3 percent or even higher" a 50 bp hike "should be considered" and that 25 bp alone would then put credibility at risk. He also argued that promised hikes must actually be carried out or markets would unwind the tightening.
- Barr (18 Nov): expects it will be appropriate to raise rates further if the economy evolves as he forecasts.
- Cook (20 Nov): dissenter, says waiting has a cost, data since September "fail to provide sufficient evidence" of disinflation. Clear hike vote.
- Jefferson (13 Nov): sees the inflation trajectory as "the more important signal"; no explicit commitment.
- Bowman (10 Nov, after CPI): "still work to do" on inflation and labour market "firming", but stresses that the benefit of additional tightening "has to be carefully weighed against its costs" for rate-sensitive sectors, and wants clarity on the effect of recent market volatility. The most hesitant voice in the pack.
- Warsh (28 Oct press conference, 16 Nov remarks): refuses forward guidance and will not prejudge; calls the coming period "information rich"; says "we don't just talk - we act"; says policy works through tightening financial conditions and that he is not uncomfortable with the tightening so far. His 16 Nov remarks list an "SI disappoints / equity valuations fall" scenario among contingencies, with no policy signal.

Net reading (inference): the stated baseline of nearly the whole Committee is one more 25 bp hike at this meeting. The Chair has kept optionality, and one or two governors emphasise financial-conditions risk.

## 2. Latest official data and the direction each pushes

Inflation - pushes toward a hike, and toward a larger one at the margin
- October CPI: headline +0.4 percent m/m, core +0.3 percent for the third month running. Broad increases (shelter 0.3, food 0.5, recreation 0.8, motor vehicle insurance 0.7, hospital services 0.8). This meets the threshold Waller named for considering 50 bp.
- September PCE: 3.6 percent y/y headline, 3.1 percent core. Governors' estimates for October: headline about 3.8 percent, core 3.2-3.3 percent - moving up, not down.
- Beige Book (data to 5 Oct): prices growing robustly in three Districts and moderately in eight, pace picking up in four, energy costs passing through supply chains.
- Offsets: energy CPI slowed to +0.7 percent from +5.2 percent; Brent fell sharply in early November on hopes of a US-Iran agreement; staff still expect inflation to step down next year; longer-term expectations described as anchored.

Employment - pushes toward a hike (removes the main reason to wait)
- October payrolls +165,000, with August and September revised up by a combined 84,000. Unemployment 4.1 percent, in a 4.1-4.3 range since March. Claims near generational lows (4-week average about 195,000).
- Soft spots: part-time for economic reasons up 339,000; long-term unemployed 27.6 percent of the unemployed; "low hire, low fire"; wage growth about 3.1 percent and decelerating (no wage-price pressure).

Activity - pushes toward a hike
- Q3 real GDP +3.4 percent annualised; real final sales to private domestic purchasers +4.9 percent; AI-related equipment investment surging and broadening.
- Offsets: housing and other rate-sensitive sectors weak; Beige Book describes only slight-to-modest growth and flat consumer spending in most Districts; Waller expects Q4 growth to back off a bit.

Financial conditions - the main force pushing toward hold
- Since late October: AI-related equity valuations down, corporate and mortgage spreads wider, CDS up for some AI-buildout firms, dollar stronger, French sovereign stress. Barr (16 Nov) refers to "the past couple of weeks" of investor questions about the AI boom. Staff judged vulnerabilities could amplify shocks "somewhat more than usual".
- Warsh frames tighter conditions as policy working, not as a reason to stop. Cook still calls conditions "too accommodative".

## 3. Strongest case for each outcome

Cut of 50 bp or more
- Only a disorderly financial event in the next two weeks does this: an AI-credit or equity collapse that impairs market functioning through the opaque off-balance-sheet financing and hedge-fund leverage channels the staff flagged, forcing an emergency easing. Even then, with headline PCE near 3.8 percent and a Chair who says the Committee "will deliver price stability - no ifs, ands, or buts", the first response would more likely be liquidity tools than the funds rate. Tail only.

Cut of 25 bp
- Same crisis path in milder form, plus a collapse in oil after a US-Iran deal giving cover. Nothing in the pack shows any participant contemplating easing; the staff put a first cut in 2028 territory via the Desk survey. Tail only.

Hold
- The Chair has given no commitment and held in October despite hot data and two dissents. The reasons cited then - durability of AI demand, financing conditions, transmission of the September hike, geopolitics - have if anything intensified: AI equities and credit sold off in November and oil dropped sharply on deal hopes. Bowman explicitly weighs the costs of more tightening. If markets deteriorate further before 9 December, or if October PCE and the November jobs report soften, a majority could decide that markets are doing the tightening for them and wait until January while keeping a hawkish statement.

Hike of 25 bp
- It is the stated baseline of "almost all participants", the SEP median, the Desk-survey expectation and (as of late October) market pricing. Every major data release since the October meeting was firm or hot: core CPI 0.3 again, 12-month PCE rising to about 3.8 percent, payrolls +165,000 with upward revisions, unemployment 4.1 percent, GDP 3.4 percent. Two members already voted to hike, a few more called October a close call, and Waller, Barr and Cook have all pointed at December. The October hold was justified by the December hike being priced; not delivering would loosen conditions and damage the "we act" credibility Warsh has built his chairmanship on. 25 bp also fits the "gradual approach" language.

Hike of 50 bp or more
- Waller's own stated trigger (core CPI around 0.3 or higher) was hit hours after he named it, and he said 25 bp alone would then risk credibility. "A couple" of participants raised more than 25 bp for this meeting in the minutes, and four September dots implied 50 bp of further 2026 tightening, which can now only be delivered in one step. Inflation is accelerating on a 12-month basis and the labour market is firming. A Chair who wants to show a break with the past could choose to "catch up" for the skipped October move.

## 4. What I am least sure of, and its effect on the spread

- The state of markets after mid-November. The pack has no market data later than the October minutes, only governors' references to falling AI equities and wider spreads. If this has become a real rout, hold rises sharply; if it is an orderly repricing (which Warsh welcomes), it does not block a hike. This is the main reason I keep hold in the mid-teens rather than under 10.
- Warsh's reaction function. He rejects forward guidance, does not submit a dot, and surprised the hawks in October. That adds variance in both directions (hold and 50), so I widen both tails relative to what the minutes alone imply.
- How many others share Waller's 50 bp view. He said "should be considered", not "I will vote for". Financial-conditions tightening, the "gradual approach" comments and the fall in oil all argue against it, so I keep 50 bp near 10 percent rather than higher.
- Data still to come before the meeting: October PCE and the November employment report (November CPI is due 10 December, after the decision). A very hot pair nudges toward 50; a weak jobs number nudges toward hold. Neither is likely to overturn 25 bp as the mode.
- Geopolitics: a US-Iran agreement would lower headline inflation pressure and modestly raise hold; escalation raises both inflation and financial-stress risk.
- Cuts require a crisis that the pack shows no sign of yet; I keep both at well under 1 percent each.
