# F06 forecast: FOMC decision of 8-9 December 2026 (change in upper bound of the target range)

Based only on the evidence pack cp/K1/pack.md (22 official documents, captured 2026-11-26). The upper bound going into the meeting is 4.00 percent (range 3-3/4 to 4 percent).

## 1. What the Committee has done and what it is signalling

**Recent actions**
- September 15-16: raised the range 25 bp to 3-3/4 to 4 percent ("removed a dose of accommodation").
- October 27-28: held, by a 10-2 vote. Cook and Hammack dissented in favour of a 25 bp hike.
- The October statement was hawkish for a hold: "Inflation remains elevated... The accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation... The Committee will deliver price stability."

**September SEP (the Committee's own baseline)**
- Median end-2026 funds rate was 4.1 percent, which is one more 25 bp hike from the current range.
- Dots for end-2026: 12 of 18 at 4.125, 4 at 4.375, 2 at 3.875. So 16 of 18 participants had at least one more hike by year-end, and December is the last meeting of the year.
- 17 of 18 saw inflation risks weighted to the upside.

**October minutes (published November 18)**
- "Almost all participants" judged that further action to raise the range, "broadly consistent with the September SEP," would likely be appropriate.
- "Some" favoured hiking in October; "a few" who supported the hold called it a close call.
- "Some" said market-implied tightening "in December and next year" was broadly consistent with their view of the appropriate path, and several saw that alignment as lowering the risk of holding in October.
- "A couple" said a move of more than 25 bp could warrant consideration in December if inflation data were unfavourable.
- Futures assigned a high probability to a 25 bp hike by year-end; the Desk survey median expected a December hike and another in March 2027.

**Speeches since the meeting**
- Waller (Nov 10, before the CPI release): "even if... the monthly rate of inflation improves in October, I am leaning toward supporting an increase... of 25 basis points" in December. If core CPI came in "around 0.3 percent or even higher," a 50 bp hike "should be considered," because 25 "would put our credibility at risk." He also said that if signalled hikes are not carried out, market rates fall and policy loosens "in an undesirable way."
- Cook (Nov 20): repeated the case for her dissent, said waiting has a cost, and said the inflation data since September "fail to provide me with sufficient evidence that inflation is returning to a sustainable path."
- Barr (Nov 18): "I expect it will be appropriate to raise rates further."
- Jefferson (Nov 13): activity and labour are solid, inflation shows "persistent price pressures," and "the trajectory of inflation [is] the more important signal." He did not commit on timing or size.
- Bowman (Nov 10): the most cautious. "There is still work to do," but the benefit of more tightening "has to be carefully weighed against its costs for interest-rate-sensitive sectors"; she wants clarity on AI financing conditions and recent market volatility.
- Warsh (Oct 28 press conference, Nov 16 remarks): declines to give forward guidance or prejudge meetings. He called the coming period "information rich," said the September hike was "starting to work some," and said financial conditions are tighter but that he is "unwilling and unable to call them 'tight.'" He prefers working through financial conditions rather than "throwing sand in the gears."

**Reading:** the Committee's own documents make a 25 bp hike in December the base case. The October hold was explained as a pause to gather information, not a change of direction, and the information that has arrived since is hawkish on balance.

## 2. Latest official data

**Inflation (pushes toward a hike, and toward the 50 bp discussion)**
- October CPI: headline +0.4 percent; core +0.3 percent for the third month running. That is the reading Waller named as his threshold for considering 50 bp. Increases were broad (shelter, recreation, medical care, motor insurance, transportation services +0.8).
- October PCE: headline +0.3 percent m/m and 3.8 percent y/y (3.7 in September); core +0.3 percent m/m and 3.3 percent y/y (3.1 in September). Both 12-month rates are moving away from target.
- Beige Book: prices up moderately, contacts expect further increases, wages picked up to "moderate" from "modest."
- Offsets: Brent fell sharply in early November on optimism about a US-Iran agreement; energy CPI slowed to +0.7 percent from +5.2; firms report limited ability to pass costs through. The Q3 quarterly PCE price indexes in the GDP release (2.1 percent headline, 2.5 percent core, annualised) look softer than the monthly data, but they were unrevised and already known.
- November CPI is published on December 10, after the decision, so no further major inflation report arrives before the meeting.

**Employment (permits a hike; no reason for restraint)**
- October payrolls +165,000, with +84,000 in revisions to August and September; unemployment 4.1 percent; average hourly earnings +0.4 percent m/m and 3.1 percent y/y.
- Initial claims near historical lows (four-week average about 195,000, per Cook).
- Soft spots: involuntary part-time work up 339,000; long-term unemployed 27.6 percent of the total; "low hire, low fire."
- The November jobs report on December 4 is the main remaining release. It would have to be very weak to change the outcome.

**Activity (pushes toward a hike, with early signs of slowing)**
- Q3 GDP revised up to 4.3 percent from 3.4; real final sales to private domestic purchasers 5.8 percent.
- More recent indicators are softer: real PCE flat in October; Beige Book shows eight districts with slight-to-moderate growth and four unchanged, consumer spending little changed, housing and construction weaker, autos softer.
- Financial conditions have tightened: higher mortgage rates, wider spreads, a drop in AI-related equity valuations "these past couple of weeks" (Barr, Nov 16), a stronger dollar, and a French sovereign repricing. This is the main argument available to doves.

## 3. Strongest case for each outcome

- **Cut of 50 bp or more.** Only an acute crisis in the next two weeks would do it: a disorderly AI-equity and credit collapse with forced deleveraging and impaired market functioning (the tail scenario in the staff's October financial stability briefing), or a major cyber or sovereign event. Even then, with core PCE at 3.3 percent the first response would be liquidity tools rather than a large cut. Negligible probability.
- **Cut of 25 bp.** The same crisis logic in a milder form, or a collapse in the November jobs report combined with a market rout. Nothing in the pack points this way; no participant is discussing cuts and the SEP shows none in 2026. Negligible probability.
- **Hold.** The Committee held in October when a majority already expected to hike later, so its willingness to wait is demonstrated. Since then financial conditions have tightened further, AI valuations have fallen, real consumption stalled in October, the Beige Book is soft, oil has dropped on hopes of an Iran deal, and tariff effects are waning. Bowman explicitly weighs the costs of more tightening. Warsh refuses forward guidance, says the September hike is beginning to work, and faces public pressure from the President. Several participants stressed that the path matters more than the date, so the hike could slide to January if markets wobble. This is the main alternative to the base case.
- **Hike of 25 bp.** This is what the September dots (16 of 18), the October minutes ("almost all participants"), market pricing and the Desk survey all pointed to. The data since have reinforced it: core PCE up to 3.3 percent, three straight 0.3 percent core CPI prints, GDP at 4.3 percent, payrolls +165,000, unemployment 4.1 percent. Two members already dissented for a hike; Waller, Barr and Cook have said so on the record; Jefferson emphasises inflation. Not delivering would ease conditions against the Committee's wishes (Waller's argument) and damage the "we will deliver price stability" message. A 25 bp step matches the gradualism that Warsh and "a couple of participants" have endorsed.
- **Hike of 50 bp or more.** Waller's stated trigger (core CPI around 0.3 percent) was met, and he said 25 alone would risk credibility. "A couple" of participants flagged a larger move in the minutes. Four participants pencilled in 4.375 percent for end-2026, which now requires 50 bp in December. Having skipped October, a 50 bp move could be framed as catching up to the intended path while inflation is accelerating and growth is running at 4 percent or more. Cook warned that delay risks "additional and larger rate increases."

## 4. What I am least sure of

1. **Hold versus hike: the Chair and financial conditions.** Warsh gives no guidance and his reaction function is only five months old. I cannot see market pricing or the scale of the AI-related selloff after mid-November. A deeper rout or a weak November jobs report could tip a cautious centre (Warsh, Bowman, perhaps Jefferson) to wait. This keeps hold well above a token probability. Against that, Barr and Cook spoke after the selloff began and still called for higher rates, and Cook described financial conditions as "too accommodative."
2. **25 versus 50.** Waller's conditional was triggered, but only he has put 50 on the table explicitly since the data. Jefferson and Bowman, both speaking after the CPI release, showed no appetite for it, and tightening financial conditions argue against a larger step. The SEP median implies only 25. A 50 bp move needs the Chair, and his rhetoric is about steady doses. Meaningful minority probability, not more.
3. **Geopolitics and oil.** A US-Iran agreement would lower headline inflation and support a look-through argument; an escalation would do the opposite. This widens both tails slightly and mostly feeds the hold probability.
4. **Thin evidence from Reserve Bank presidents.** The pack has no post-meeting speeches from Williams, Logan, Kashkari, Paulson or Hammack. Hammack's dissent and the minutes' counts suggest the presidents lean hawkish, which supports the base case, but I cannot verify it.

Net effect: the direction is clear, since no cut case exists outside a crisis. Uncertainty about timing and size keeps roughly a quarter of the probability away from the 25 bp base case, split between hold (slightly larger, because the Committee has just demonstrated patience and conditions have tightened) and 50 bp (because the data met a governor's stated trigger).
