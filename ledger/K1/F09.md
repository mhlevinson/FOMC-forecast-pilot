# F09 forecast: FOMC decision of 8-9 December 2026 (change in upper bound of the target range)

Source: the evidence pack cp/K1/pack.md only (22 official documents, captured 2026-11-26). The upper bound in effect before the meeting is 4.00 percent (target range 3-3/4 to 4 percent).

## 1. What the Committee did recently and what it signals about December

**Recent actions**

- September 15-16: raised the target range by 25 bp to 3-3/4 to 4 percent ("removed a dose of accommodation", in Chairman Warsh's words).
- October 27-28: held at 3-3/4 to 4 percent on a 10-2 vote. Cook and Hammack dissented in favour of a 25 bp hike.

**September SEP**

- Median end-2026 funds rate midpoint is 4.1 percent, i.e. one more 25 bp hike in 2026. December is the only meeting left.
- Dots for end-2026: 12 at 4.125, 4 at 4.375, 2 at 3.875. So 16 of 18 participants had at least one more hike this year, and 4 had two.
- 17 of 18 saw inflation risks weighted to the upside.

**October minutes (released Nov 18)**

- "Almost all participants" judged that further action to raise the range, "broadly consistent with the September Summary of Economic Projections", would likely be appropriate.
- "Some" participants favoured hiking in October; "a few" who supported the hold called it a close call.
- "Some" said market expectations of tightening "in December and next year" matched their view of the appropriate path, and several saw that alignment as what made the October hold low-risk.
- "A couple" noted that more than 25 bp "could warrant consideration at the next meeting if incoming inflation data were unfavorable."
- Futures assigned "high probabilities" to a 25 bp hike by year-end; the Desk survey median had one hike in December and another in March 2027.
- Counter-currents: "a couple" favoured a gradual approach; a majority noted tighter financial conditions since September; almost all commented on AI-related financial stability risks.

**Statement and press conference**

- The statement said "the accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance" and "The Committee will deliver price stability."
- Warsh refused forward guidance and would not prejudge December. He stressed "we don't just talk—we act," called the intermeeting period "information rich," and said financial conditions are tighter but that he is "unwilling and unable to call them 'tight.'"

**Speeches since the meeting**

| Speaker (date) | Signal |
|---|---|
| Waller (Nov 10) | Would support 25 bp in December even if October CPI improved. If core CPI came in "around 0.3 percent or even higher," a 50 bp hike "should be considered"; "Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk." He also said it matters that "promised hikes are implemented in a reasonable period of time." |
| Bowman (Nov 10) | Most cautious. The benefit of more tightening "has to be carefully weighed against its costs for interest-rate-sensitive sectors." But also "there is still work to do," inflation pressures persisting and broadening, labor market firming. |
| Jefferson (Nov 13) | Inflation trajectory gives "the more important signal"; October data "consistent with well above-target inflation." No explicit commitment. |
| Barr (Nov 18) | "I expect it will be appropriate to raise rates further." |
| Cook (Nov 20) | Restated her dissent; "waiting to act... has a cost"; sceptical one hike is enough; larger hikes may be needed later if the Committee falls behind. |
| Warsh (Nov 16) | Opening remarks on productivity and "super intelligence" only; no policy signal. |

Reading: the October hold was a pause inside a tightening path, made comfortable by a priced-in December hike. The official signals point clearly to a 25 bp hike in December, with a visible hawkish tail toward 50 and a smaller dovish tail toward another hold.

## 2. What the latest data show

**Inflation: pushes toward hiking**

- October CPI (Nov 10): headline +0.4 percent, core +0.3 percent for the third straight month. That meets the threshold Waller named for considering 50 bp. Gasoline is up 37.4 percent over 12 months.
- October PCE (Nov 25): headline +0.3 percent m/m and 3.8 percent y/y (up from 3.6-3.7 in September). Core +0.3 percent m/m and 3.3 percent y/y (up from 3.1).
- Inflation is moving further from 2 percent and broadening: more than 70 percent of core services components were above 3 percent in September (Waller).
- Mild offsets: Brent fell sharply in early November on hopes of a U.S.-Iran agreement; refined-product prices retraced some gains; tariff effects are waning; used car prices fell 1.2 percent.
- The November CPI is due December 10, after the decision, so it cannot affect it.

**Employment: permits hiking**

- October payrolls +165,000, with +84,000 in revisions to August and September.
- Unemployment 4.1 percent; average hourly earnings +0.4 percent m/m and 3.1 percent y/y; initial claims near historical lows.
- Soft spots: part-time for economic reasons +339,000; long-term unemployed 27.6 percent of the unemployed; "low hire, low fire."
- The November employment report (Dec 4) is the main release still to come.

**Activity: strong in the rear-view mirror, some recent softening**

- Q3 real GDP revised up to 4.3 percent (from 3.4); real final sales to private domestic purchasers 5.8 percent.
- More recent data are softer. October real PCE was flat and the saving rate rose to 4.1 percent.
- The November Beige Book reported consumer spending "little changed," four districts with no growth, weaker housing and construction, and softer auto sales. It also reported wages and prices rising moderately.
- Financial conditions tightened: AI equity valuations dropped, credit spreads widened, mortgage rates rose. Barr (Nov 16) referred to "the past couple of weeks" of investor questioning of the AI boom.
- These are the main arguments for caution, but so far they read as "policy is working" (Warsh), not as stress.

Net: inflation data argue for at least 25 bp and put 50 bp on the table. Labor and GDP data remove the employment-side reason to wait. Softening consumption, tighter financial conditions, lower oil and financial-stability worries argue against going bigger than 25 and give a slim case for waiting.

## 3. Strongest case for each outcome

**Cut of 50 bp or more.** Only a sudden systemic event before December 9 would do it: a disorderly unwinding of AI-related leverage (hedge fund deleveraging, off-balance-sheet obligations above $3 trillion, impaired Treasury market functioning) severe enough to force emergency easing. Nothing in the pack shows this; the staff judged the financial system resilient. With inflation at 3.8 percent the bar is extremely high.

**Cut of 25 bp.** A milder version of the same: a sharp AI equity and credit correction plus a very weak November jobs report, set against collapsing oil prices after a U.S.-Iran deal, with political pressure on the Board in the background. Nobody on the Committee is asking for cuts; every dot for end-2026 is at or above the current rate.

**Hold.** The Committee already held once in October when a hike was arguable, citing the value of more information, and the Chair refuses to pre-commit. Since then:

- financial conditions have tightened further and AI equities sold off;
- oil fell sharply on peace hopes;
- real PCE was flat in October and the Beige Book softened;
- Bowman explicitly weighs the costs to rate-sensitive sectors;
- the minutes show strong concern about AI-related financial fragility.

If market turmoil intensified in early December or the November payroll number were poor, the "accumulate more information" logic could be reused, with markets repricing beforehand as they did before October.

**Hike of 25 bp (base case).**

- It is the SEP median path, and "almost all" participants see further hikes as appropriate.
- "Some" participants said market pricing of a December hike matched their own view.
- Two members already dissented for a hike; Waller and Barr have effectively pre-announced support; Jefferson says inflation is the more important signal.
- The data since October are uniformly unhelpful on inflation (core PCE 3.3 percent and rising) and solid on jobs and GDP.
- Waller's point that promised hikes must be delivered or conditions will ease undesirably makes a second consecutive hold costly for credibility after the "will deliver price stability" language.
- 25 bp fits the gradualism some participants prefer and the financial-stability caution that argues against a larger step.

**Hike of 50 bp or more.**

- Waller's stated condition (core CPI around 0.3 or higher) was met, and he said 25 alone "would put our credibility at risk."
- "A couple" of participants in October already flagged more than 25 for December if inflation data were unfavourable, and they were (core PCE up from 3.1 to 3.3, headline 3.8).
- Four participants' September dots had two more hikes by year-end, which now requires 50 in December.
- Cook warns about falling behind the curve.
- GDP was revised to 4.3 percent and payrolls were strong.
- A Chair who wants to demonstrate a "new monetary medicine" could choose a decisive move, and a strong November jobs report would add to the case.

## 4. What I am least sure of

- **The Chair's reaction function.** Warsh gives no forward guidance, and the October hold shows the Committee will not hike mechanically. I cannot tell from the pack whether he leans toward demonstrating resolve (25, or even 50) or toward leaning on tightening financial conditions to do the work (hold). This widens both tails around 25.
- **Financial markets in late November and early December.** The pack shows an AI-related equity and credit repricing under way but gives no market data after mid-November and no current futures pricing. A disorderly move is the main route to a hold, and the only route to a cut. This keeps the hold probability in double digits.
- **How many colleagues share Waller's 50 bp view.** The pack shows "a couple" in October, four high dots in September, and two hike dissenters. That is a minority; a majority for 50 would need the Chair. Offsetting considerations are the fall in oil, flat real consumption, tighter conditions and the financial-stability discussion. I keep 50 at roughly one in ten.
- **The November employment report (Dec 4)** and any geopolitical shift (an Iran deal lowering oil further, or escalation). A weak report raises the hold probability; a strong one raises 50.
- **A data-quality note.** The GDP release in the pack lists Q3 PCE price inflation at 2.1 percent and core at 2.5 percent annualised, which sits oddly beside the monthly PCE figures. I rely on the monthly release and officials' own characterisations, which are consistent with each other.

Overall, the direction of travel is unusually well signalled for a Committee that avoids guidance, which concentrates probability on a 25 bp hike. The uncertainties above spread about a quarter of the mass to hold and to a 50 bp hike, with cuts as remote tail outcomes.
