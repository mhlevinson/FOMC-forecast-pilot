# F07 - FOMC 8-9 December 2026: change in the upper bound of the target range

Bottom line: a 25 basis point hike (upper bound 4.00 to 4.25 percent) is the clear base case, at about 80 percent. The main alternative is a second consecutive hold (about 13 percent), followed by a 50 basis point hike (about 6 percent). Cuts are tail outcomes only.

All facts below come from the evidence pack (cutoff 2026-11-23). Inferences are marked as such.

## 1. What the Committee did and what it signals

Facts
- September 15-16: raised the range by 25 bp to 3-3/4 to 4 percent ("removed a dose of accommodation").
- October 27-28: held at 3-3/4 to 4 percent on a 10-2 vote. Cook and Hammack dissented in favor of a 25 bp hike. The statement said more information "will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate" and "The Committee will deliver price stability."
- September SEP dots for end-2026: 12 participants at 4.125, 4 at 4.375, 2 at 3.875. The current midpoint is 3.875, and December is the last meeting of the year, so 16 of 18 participants had at least one more hike in 2026.
- October minutes: "almost all participants" judged that further action to raise the range, "broadly consistent with the September Summary of Economic Projections", would likely be appropriate. "Some" favored hiking already in October (more than the two dissenting voters), "a few" of the holders called the hold a close call, and "a couple" said more than 25 bp could warrant consideration in December if inflation data were unfavorable. Some participants said market pricing of a December hike matched their own view of the appropriate path.
- Market pricing at the October meeting: little chance of an October move, "high probabilities assigned to a 25 basis point hike by year-end"; Desk survey median expected a December hike.
- Speeches since the meeting:
  - Waller (Nov 10): would support a 25 bp hike in December even if October CPI improved; if core CPI came in "around 0.3 percent or even higher", a 50 bp hike "should be considered". He also argued that priced-in hikes must actually be delivered "in a reasonable period of time" or financial conditions would loosen undesirably.
  - Barr (Nov 18): expects it will be appropriate to raise rates further if the economy evolves as he forecasts.
  - Cook (Nov 20): repeats the case for hiking; says post-September inflation data give no evidence of a return to a sustainable path; "waiting to act ... has a cost".
  - Jefferson (Nov 13): data since the meeting show "persistent price pressures"; inflation is "the more important signal" for policy. No explicit commitment.
  - Bowman (Nov 10): "still work to do" on inflation, but stresses that financial conditions have tightened and that "the benefit of additional policy tightening has to be carefully weighed against its costs". The most cautious voice in the pack.
  - Warsh (Oct 28 press conference, Nov 16 remarks): rejects forward guidance and "prejudging meetings", but describes the interval before December as "information rich" and says "we don't just talk - we act". Nothing on near-term policy in the Nov 16 remarks.

Inference
- The October hold reads as a pause inside a tightening sequence, not as the end of it. The Committee effectively pre-positioned December as the delivery date: the dots, the minutes language, the dissents, and Waller's explicit statement all point the same way. Only Bowman's remarks lean toward patience.

## 2. Latest official data and which way each pushes

Inflation - pushes toward a hike, and marginally toward a larger one
- October CPI: headline +0.4 percent, core +0.3 percent for the third straight month (Aug, Sep, Oct). Increases were broad (shelter, recreation, medical care, motor vehicle insurance, transportation services +0.8, food +0.5). This meets the condition Waller named for considering 50 bp.
- September PCE: +0.5 percent m/m, 3.6 percent y/y; core +0.3 percent m/m, 3.1 percent y/y. Officials' estimates for October: headline about 3.8 percent, core 3.2 to 3.3 percent (Cook, Bowman). So core is still drifting up, 67 months above target.
- Offsetting detail: energy CPI slowed to +0.7 percent after +5.2 percent; Bowman reports Brent fell sharply in early November on hopes of a US-Iran agreement; Q3 quarterly core PCE was 2.5 percent annualized.

Employment - permits a hike, does not demand one
- October payrolls +165,000, with +84,000 in upward revisions to August and September; unemployment 4.1 percent, in a 4.1 to 4.3 percent range since March; average hourly earnings +0.4 percent m/m, 3.1 percent y/y.
- Soft spots: part-time for economic reasons up 339,000; long-term unemployed 27.6 percent of the unemployed; "low hire, low fire". Wages are not a source of inflation pressure.

Activity - pushes toward a hike, with a financial-conditions caveat
- Q3 real GDP +3.4 percent (advance); final sales to private domestic purchasers +4.9 percent; AI-related investment booming and broadening.
- Caveats: real PCE was flat in September and real DPI fell 0.3 percent; the saving rate is 3.8 percent; the October Beige Book showed only slight-to-modest growth and consumer spending carried by high-income households; housing is weak.
- Financial conditions: tighter since September - higher mortgage rates, wider credit spreads (especially AI and high-yield), lower tech equity valuations, stronger dollar. Barr (Nov 16) refers to investor doubts about the AI boom "these past couple of weeks"; the November Financial Stability Report flags AI debt and hedge fund leverage. Cook (Nov 20) nonetheless judges financial conditions "too accommodative".

## 3. Strongest case for each outcome

Cut of 50 bp or more
- Would require an acute financial accident before December 9 - a disorderly AI-equity and credit unwind with fire sales through leveraged hedge funds and prime brokers, of the kind the staff's tail scenarios and the FSR describe, severe enough to impair market functioning. Even then, the minutes show participants prefer liquidity tools and distinguish market-functioning actions from monetary accommodation, and inflation is at 3.8 percent. Extremely unlikely in a 16-day window.

Cut of 25 bp
- Same crisis path in a milder form, or a sharp growth scare combined with a Middle East settlement that collapses energy prices. Nothing in the Committee's communication contemplates it; no participant has a 2026 dot below the current range.

Hold
- The October majority held precisely to assess "the durability of AI-related investment demand" and "the evolution of financing conditions". Both have moved in the cautious direction since: AI equities and credit have repriced, the FSR highlights amplification risk, and Bowman explicitly weighs the cost of further tightening. Warsh's framework treats tighter financial conditions as the main transmission channel, so a market-driven tightening could be argued to substitute for a hike. Oil has dropped on deal hopes, which would lower headline inflation. Warsh refuses to pre-commit, and the Committee already held once against the dots. A deepening selloff, a weak November jobs report (Dec 4) or a benign October PCE (Nov 25) could tip a divided Committee to wait until January.

Hike of 25 bp
- Almost every signal converges here: 16 of 18 September dots, "almost all participants" in the minutes, two existing dissents for a hike, more participants who wanted one in October, Waller's explicit support even on better data, Barr's stated expectation, market and Desk-survey pricing, and data since October that came in on the hawkish side (payrolls +165,000 with upward revisions, a third straight 0.3 percent core CPI, core PCE rising to 3.2-3.3 percent, GDP 3.4 percent). Waller's logic that priced-in hikes must be delivered or conditions loosen is a direct argument against a second hold. Skipping again would undercut the statement's own line that the Committee "will deliver price stability". A 25 bp step also matches the "gradual approach" and "dose" language.

Hike of 50 bp or more
- Waller's stated trigger was met: core CPI was 0.3 percent again, and he said that in that case 50 bp "should be considered" and that 25 bp "would put our credibility at risk". A couple of participants raised more than 25 bp at the October meeting; Cook warns that delay may require "larger rate increases" later; the hold in October means the Committee is one step behind its own September path, and a few dots were already at 4.375 for year-end. A 50 bp move would land exactly on those four dots. Against this: the majority would not even do 25 bp in October, financial conditions are tightening with AI-related market stress, energy prices have eased, markets price 25, and "considered" is not "supported".

## 4. What I am least sure of

- The path of AI-related equity and credit markets between now and December 9. The pack shows a repricing under way but gives no levels and nothing after November 20. This is the main source of hold risk and the only source of cut risk; it widens the left side of the distribution.
- Warsh's own reaction function. He submits no dot, rejects guidance, already surprised hawkish expectations in October, and is under visible political pressure. His weight on financial conditions as the transmission channel cuts both ways. This keeps hold in the low double digits rather than single digits.
- How many participants share Waller's 50 bp view after the CPI print. No other official in the pack endorsed it after November 10, and Cook's November 20 speech, though hawkish, did not call for 50. This keeps the right tail modest.
- Two releases still to come before the meeting (October PCE on November 25, November employment on December 4), plus geopolitics (a US-Iran agreement or an escalation). These could shift the odds a few points in either direction but are unlikely to reverse the base case.
- Net effect: the signals for a hike are unusually explicit, which narrows the distribution around +25 bp; the financial-stability backdrop and the chair's opacity stop me from going above roughly 80 percent.

- Cut 25 bp: 0.5 percent
- Hold: 13.0 percent
- Hike 25 bp: 80.0 percent
- Hike 50 bp or more: 6.3 percent
