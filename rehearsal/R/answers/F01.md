# F01 - FOMC 8-9 December 2026: change in the upper bound of the target range

Bottom line: a 25 basis point hike (upper bound 4.00 -> 4.25 percent) is the clear base case, at about 80 percent. A hold is the main alternative (about 11 percent), a 50 point hike is a live but minority tail (about 8 percent), and cuts are near-impossible (about 1 percent combined).

Source: only /home/claude/cp/R/pack.md (21 official documents, cutoff 2026-11-23T07:06:35Z). "Doc N" refers to the pack's numbering. Facts and inferences are labelled.

## 1. What the Committee did and what it signals

Facts
- September 15-16: raised the range 25 bp to 3-3/4 to 4 percent (Docs 1, 6). The September SEP median for end-2026 is 4.1 percent, one more 25 bp step. Dots for end-2026: 12 at 4.125, 4 at 4.375, 2 at 3.875 (Doc 5). December is the only meeting left in 2026, so 16 of 18 participants in September had the range at least 25 bp higher by year-end.
- October 27-28: held at 3-3/4 to 4 percent by 10-2; Cook and Hammack dissented for a 25 bp hike (Docs 1, 2). Statement: "The accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance... The Committee will deliver price stability."
- October minutes (Doc 1): "almost all participants judged that... further action to raise the target range - broadly consistent with the September Summary of Economic Projections - would likely be appropriate." "Some participants" favored hiking in October already; "a few" who held called it a close call; "a couple" said more than 25 bp "could warrant consideration at the next meeting if incoming inflation data were unfavorable." Some participants said market pricing of tightening "in December and next year" matched their own view, and saw that alignment as what made the October hold low-risk.
- Market pricing at the October meeting: high probability of a 25 bp hike by year-end; Desk survey median had one hike in December (Doc 1).
- Chairman Warsh (Doc 4): refuses forward guidance and will not "prejudge" meetings, but called the coming period "information rich," said "we don't just talk - we act," and said tighter financial conditions are "policy working," not a reason for discomfort. His 16 November speech (Doc 11) says nothing on near-term policy.
- Post-meeting speakers:
  - Waller, 10 Nov (Doc 16): tightening needed "sooner rather than later"; would support 25 bp in December even if October inflation improved; if October core CPI were "around 0.3 percent or even higher," a 50 bp hike "should be considered" and "hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk." He also argued the October hold was only acceptable because promised hikes get delivered "in a reasonable period of time."
  - Cook, 20 Nov (Doc 6): financial conditions "too accommodative," waiting "has a cost," post-September data "fail to provide me with sufficient evidence that inflation is returning to a sustainable path." She speaks of a 1/4 point preference, not 50.
  - Barr, 18 Nov (Doc 9): "I expect it will be appropriate to raise rates further."
  - Jefferson, 13 and 19 Nov (Docs 13, 8): inflation readings show "persistent price pressures"; inflation trajectory is "the more important signal."
  - Bowman, 10 Nov (Doc 15): the most cautious. Inflation is persisting and "there is still work to do," but "the benefit of additional policy tightening has to be carefully weighed against its costs for interest-rate-sensitive sectors," and she wants clarity on market volatility and AI financing.

Inference
- The Committee skipped October on the explicit understanding that December was the likely delivery date. Every monetary-policy speaker since then except Bowman points to a hike, and Bowman does not argue against one. The center of gravity is 25 bp.

## 2. Latest official data and which way each pushes

Inflation - pushes toward a hike, and toward the upper tail
- October CPI (Doc 19): headline +0.4 percent m/m; core +0.3 percent, the third 0.3 in a row. Shelter +0.3, transportation services +0.8, recreation +0.8, food +0.5. This is exactly the reading Waller named as the trigger for considering 50 bp.
- September PCE (Doc 20): headline +0.5 m/m, 3.6 percent y/y; core +0.3 m/m, 3.1 percent y/y.
- Officials' October estimates: headline PCE about 3.8 percent, core 3.2 to 3.3 percent (Docs 6, 15) - core rising, not falling. Cook: price increases are broad (airfare, appliances, furniture, hotels, restaurants).
- Beige Book (Doc 17, data through 5 Oct): price growth moderate to robust in 11 of 12 Districts, pace picking up in four, fuel surcharges passing through.
- Offsets: Brent fell sharply in early November on hopes of a US-Iran agreement and refined-product prices retraced (Docs 15, 16); tariff effects are waning; Q3 quarterly PCE prices were softer (2.1 headline, 2.5 core annualized, Doc 21).

Employment - pushes toward a hike (removes the main excuse to wait)
- October jobs (Doc 18): payrolls +165,000, prior two months revised up 84,000, unemployment 4.1 percent, average hourly earnings +0.4 percent m/m and 3.1 percent y/y. Claims near historical lows (Doc 6).
- Offsets: low-hire/low-fire, long-term unemployed at 27.6 percent of the unemployed, involuntary part-time up 339,000. These argue against overdoing it, not against 25 bp.

Activity - pushes toward a hike
- Q3 GDP +3.4 percent, real final sales to private domestic purchasers +4.9 percent (Doc 21). Capex broadening.
- Offsets: real PCE flat in September and real DPI -0.3 percent (Doc 20); housing and rate-sensitive sectors weak; Beige Book describes mostly slight to modest growth; Waller expects Q4 growth to back off.

Financial conditions - the one factor pushing toward a hold, and against 50
- AI-related equities fell and credit spreads widened in late October and November; AI financing costs rose (Docs 1, 12, 15). The November Financial Stability Report flags AI debt, off-balance-sheet obligations above 3 trillion dollars and hedge fund leverage (Doc 12).
- But Warsh frames tighter conditions as intended, Cook still calls conditions too easy, and Waller refers to a "booming stock market." Inference: the selloff to date is a repricing, not a stress event.

## 3. Strongest case for each outcome

- Cut of 50 or more: only a sudden systemic break in the next two weeks - a disorderly AI-debt and hedge fund deleveraging that impairs market functioning (the tail scenario the staff presented in October), or a major geopolitical or cyber shock. Even then the first response would more likely be liquidity tools than a 50 bp cut with core PCE above 3 percent.
- Cut of 25: the same kind of shock at lower intensity. Nothing in the pack shows any participant contemplating easing; 17 of 18 see inflation risks to the upside.
- Hold: Warsh insists policy is "not about some fixed individual date on a calendar" and rejects forward guidance, and he already held once when the press thought most colleagues were on board. If the AI equity and credit repricing deepens into early December, if an Iran agreement pulls energy prices down sharply, or if the October PCE (25 Nov) and November jobs report (4 Dec) come in soft, Bowman's cost-benefit argument could carry a majority to "let the tightening in financial conditions do the work" for one more meeting. Political pressure after the midterms is an additional unquantifiable factor.
- Hike of 25: the Committee's own stated baseline ("almost all participants"), the SEP median, market and Desk-survey expectations, and a named supporter in every post-meeting policy speech. Data since October are firmer on both inflation and jobs. Waller's credibility argument - undelivered hikes ease conditions "in an undesirable way" - makes a second skip costly. 25 bp also balances the hawks against financial-stability concerns.
- Hike of 50 or more: Waller's explicit condition (core CPI around 0.3) was met the same day he stated it. A couple of participants raised more than 25 in October, four September dots implied 50 bp more by year-end, there are two standing dissenters, and core PCE is drifting up to 3.2-3.3 percent. A chair who wants to show "we will deliver price stability - no ifs, ands, or buts" after skipping October could prefer to catch up in one step.

## 4. What I am least sure of

- Warsh's reaction function. He does not guide, does not submit a dot, and has only five months of record (one hike, one hold in the pack). This is the main reason I do not put the 25 bp hike above about 80 percent, and it fattens both the hold and the 50 tails.
- How bad the AI market repricing is as of late November. The pack only gives officials' descriptions through 10-20 November; no price data. A deeper break raises hold and cuts 50; stabilization does the opposite. This widens the hold probability.
- Whether Waller's 50 bp view has followers. No speaker after the CPI release (Barr, Jefferson, Cook) endorsed 50, and Cook - a dissenter - still talks in quarter-point terms. That keeps 50 in single digits despite the trigger being met.
- Data still to come before the meeting: October PCE and Q3 GDP second estimate (25 Nov), November employment (4 Dec). November CPI lands 10 December, after the decision, so it cannot move it. A hot PCE print shifts mass from hold toward 50; a soft jobs report shifts some from 25 toward hold.
- Energy and geopolitics: an Iran deal would lower headline inflation but leaves core above 3 percent, so I treat it as a modest hold-side factor only.

Net effect: these uncertainties widen the distribution around the 25 bp hike on both sides, slightly more toward hold than toward 50, and leave the cut outcomes as pure shock tails.
