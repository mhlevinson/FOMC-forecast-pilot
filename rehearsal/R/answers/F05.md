# F05 - FOMC decision, 8-9 December 2026

Bottom line: a 25 basis point hike is the most likely outcome by a wide margin (about three in four). The realistic alternatives are a second hold (about one in eight) and a 50 basis point hike (about one in ten). Cuts are tail events only.

Everything below comes from the evidence pack (21 official documents, captured 2026-11-23). Facts and inferences are labelled where the difference matters.

## 1. What the Committee did and what it is signalling

Facts:
- September 2026: raised the target range by 25 bp to 3.75-4.00 percent. The September SEP dot plot for end-2026 had 12 of 18 participants at 4.125, 4 at 4.375 and 2 at 3.875. So 16 of 18 projected at least one more hike in 2026, and December is the last 2026 meeting.
- October 27-28: held at 3.75-4.00 on a 10-2 vote. Cook and Hammack dissented in favour of a 25 bp hike. The minutes say "some participants" favoured hiking at that meeting, and "a few" of the holders called it a close call.
- October minutes on the path ahead: "almost all participants" judged further increases "broadly consistent with the September SEP" would likely be appropriate. "Some" said market pricing of tightening in December and next year matched their own view. "A couple" said more than 25 bp could warrant consideration at the next meeting if inflation data were unfavourable.
- Market pricing as reported in the minutes: high probability of a 25 bp hike by year-end; the Desk survey median expected a December hike.
- Statement language: "The Committee will deliver price stability."
- Warsh (Oct 28 press conference): refuses forward guidance and will not prejudge December, but called the coming interval "information rich", said "we don't just talk - we act", and said tighter financial conditions show "policy is working".
- Speeches after the October meeting:
  - Waller (Nov 10, before the CPI release): would support 25 bp in December even if October inflation improved; if core CPI came in "around 0.3 percent or even higher", 50 bp "should be considered" and 25 "would put our credibility at risk". He also said promised hikes must be delivered "in a reasonable period of time" or markets will unwind the tightening.
  - Cook (Nov 20): repeats her dissent, says financial conditions are too accommodative, waiting has a cost, and the data since September give no evidence of disinflation. No size mentioned.
  - Barr (Nov 18): "I expect it will be appropriate to raise rates further."
  - Jefferson (Nov 13): inflation trajectory is "the more important signal"; data since the meeting show "persistent price pressures". No commitment.
  - Bowman (Nov 10): "still work to do" on inflation, labour market "firming", but stresses that financial conditions have tightened and that the benefit of more tightening must be "carefully weighed against its costs" for rate-sensitive sectors.

Inference: the December voters are Warsh, Williams, Barr, Bowman, Cook, Hammack, Jefferson, Kashkari, Logan, Paulson, Powell, Waller. Cook, Hammack, Waller and Barr are on record for higher rates. Jefferson leans that way. No official in the pack argues for holding in December. A majority for at least 25 bp looks available without the Chair having to push.

## 2. What the data show

Inflation - pushes toward a hike, and is the reason 50 is on the table:
- October CPI: headline +0.4 percent m/m; core +0.3 percent for the third straight month. Shelter +0.3, transportation services +0.8, recreation +0.8, food +0.5. Used cars -1.2 was the main offset.
- September PCE: +0.5 m/m, 3.6 percent y/y; core +0.3 m/m, 3.1 percent y/y (BEA; Waller's speech quotes 3.7 for the headline, a minor discrepancy).
- Officials' translation of October CPI/PPI into PCE: headline about 3.8 percent, core 3.2 (Cook) to 3.3 (Bowman). That is the wrong direction against a 2 percent target and above the September SEP core median of 3.4 only in trajectory, not yet in level.
- Breadth: Waller says more than 70 percent of core services components were running above 3 percent; the Beige Book has three Districts at "robust" price growth and four reporting acceleration.
- Partial offsets: Q3 quarterly PCE price index was only 2.1 percent annualised (core 2.5) after a very hot Q2; Brent fell sharply in early November on hopes of a US-Iran agreement; wage growth is a moderate 3.1 percent y/y; tariff effects are waning.

Employment - removes any reason to wait:
- October payrolls +165,000, with +84,000 of upward revisions to August and September. Unemployment 4.1 percent, in a 4.1-4.3 range since March. Claims near historic lows.
- Soft spots (low hiring, long-term unemployed at 27.6 percent, +339,000 involuntary part-time) are noted by Waller and Bowman as structural worries, not as reasons to hold.

Activity - pushes toward a hike:
- Q3 real GDP +3.4 percent; final sales to private domestic purchasers +4.9 percent; investment near a double-digit pace.
- Offsets: housing and rate-sensitive sectors weak; real PCE flat in September and real disposable income -0.3 percent; Beige Book (through Oct 5) describes mostly slight to modest growth.

Financial conditions - the one channel that argues for caution:
- AI equity valuations fell, credit spreads widened (sharply for data-centre high yield), mortgage rates rose, the dollar strengthened, France's sovereign spreads hit 2011 levels, and Binance suffered a large cyber heist. Barr (Nov 16) refers to "the past couple of weeks" of investor doubts about the AI boom.
- But Cook (Nov 20) still calls conditions "too accommodative", and Waller refers to a "booming stock market". Inference: the tightening so far is the kind the Committee welcomes, not a disorderly one.

## 3. Strongest case for each outcome

Cut of 50 bp or more: a disorderly AI-debt unwind in the next two weeks that impairs Treasury or funding markets (the tail scenario in the staff's October financial stability briefing), severe enough to override 3.8 percent inflation. Nothing in the pack shows this happening. Even in such an event, liquidity tools would come before rate cuts.

Cut of 25 bp: the same shock in milder form plus a collapse in oil after a Middle East settlement, letting the Committee call inflation a fading relative-price shock. With core PCE rising and every speaker focused on inflation, this needs a break in the data that has not occurred.

Hold: the Committee already chose patience once despite strong data, and the reasons it gave (durability of AI demand, financing conditions, transmission of the September hike, geopolitics) have all moved in a "tightening is happening without us" direction since - AI equities down, spreads wider, oil lower on deal hopes. Bowman voices exactly this cost-benefit argument. Warsh dislikes being tied to the SEP or to a calendar date and said stopping inflation "is not about some fixed individual date". A sharper market sell-off or a soft November jobs report (Dec 4) could tip a centre that called October "a close call" only among "a few".

Hike of 25 bp: almost all participants said in October that further hikes were likely appropriate; 16 of 18 September dots implied one more move in 2026; markets and the Desk survey expect December; the data since have been unambiguously on the hawkish side (core CPI 0.3 three months running, payrolls +165,000, GDP 3.4 percent, core PCE drifting up). Waller, Cook, Barr and, by her dissent, Hammack are explicit; Jefferson leans the same way; nobody is arguing for a hold. Waller's credibility point binds the Chair too: skipping again would ease conditions against the Committee's wishes. A 25 bp step also fits the "gradual approach" a couple of participants favoured and is the least disruptive option amid AI-market jitters.

Hike of 50 bp or more: Waller set a public test - core CPI "around 0.3 percent or even higher" - and October met it. He said 25 alone would then risk credibility. "A couple" of participants had already floated more than 25 bp in October. A hot October PCE (Nov 25) plus a strong November jobs report could bring enough of the Committee along, especially those who regret the October skip and see 50 as making up for it.

## 4. What I am least sure of

- Warsh's reaction function. He has chaired few meetings, gives no guidance, and already paused once when most observers expected continued tightening. This is the main reason hold is not in low single digits. It widens the hold probability.
- How many others stand with Waller on 50. Cook, Barr and Jefferson all spoke after the CPI release and none asked for a larger move; Bowman is openly wary of over-tightening. That keeps 50 near one in ten rather than one in five. Financial-stability concerns about AI leverage cut the same way.
- The size of the November AI-related sell-off. The pack describes it only qualitatively. If it has deepened since the last speech, hold gains; if it has faded, 50 gains slightly. This widens both tails around 25.
- Data still to come before the decision: October PCE (Nov 25), Q3 GDP second estimate (Nov 25), November employment (Dec 4). November CPI lands Dec 10, after the decision. These can move the split between 25 and 50 more than between hike and hold.
- Geopolitics: a US-Iran agreement would lower headline inflation prospects but, per Bowman and Waller, energy costs normalise slowly; I treat this as a modest hold-side nudge only.
- Cuts: I see no path in the documents; I keep them non-zero only for an unforeseen market accident.

Net: about 86 percent on a hike of some size, with 25 bp the clear mode; about 13 percent on a hold; under 1 percent on any cut.
